Complete History

Reformed Theology: From Wycliffe to Today

How one monk with a hammer, one French lawyer in exile, and a printing press recovered the gospel — and changed the world.

Why the history matters

Reformed theology is not a novelty. It is a recovery. The Reformers did not invent new doctrines — they excavated old ones buried under centuries of institutional accretion. When Luther read Romans, he did not find a new gospel. He found the one Paul wrote. That gospel had been obscured, not lost. The Reformation was an act of recovery: the gospel recovered from the religious economy of merit, Scripture recovered from the Latin-only mediation of the priesthood, and the church recovered as the body of Christ rather than a political hierarchy.

Understanding this history is not an academic exercise. It is how we know that what we believe is not a 16th-century invention but the faith once delivered to the saints (Jude 1:3). Every generation must recover it again.

The Full Timeline

c. 1330–1384

John Wycliffe — The Morning Star

Oxford theologian. First complete English Bible translation. Rejected transubstantiation and papal supremacy. Declared: "The authority of the Bible is greater than the authority of the church." Condemned posthumously; bones exhumed and burned in 1428. His Lollard followers carried his teachings underground for generations.

c. 1369–1415

Jan Hus — The Bohemian Reformer

Czech priest influenced by Wycliffe. Preached against indulgences, demanded communion in both kinds. Burned at the stake at the Council of Constance (1415) despite an imperial safe-conduct. His last words: "Today you cook a goose, but from the ashes a swan will arise" — a century later, Luther claimed the prophecy.

1516

Erasmus — The Greek New Testament

Desiderius Erasmus publishes the first printed Greek New Testament. For the first time, scholars can compare Jerome's Latin Vulgate against the original Greek. The textual foundation for Luther's translation is laid. The gap between the apostolic church and the institutional church becomes visible on the page.

1517

Luther's 95 Theses

October 31 — Martin Luther, Augustinian monk and theology professor at Wittenberg, nails 95 theses against indulgences to the castle church door. The printing press transforms a local academic dispute into a continental crisis within months. Luther expected a debate. He got a Reformation.

1521

Diet of Worms

Luther before Emperor Charles V: "Unless I am convinced by the testimony of the Scriptures or by clear reason... I am bound by the Scriptures I have quoted. My conscience is captive to the Word of God. Here I stand. I cannot do otherwise. God help me. Amen." The formal break is now irreversible.

1536

Calvin's Institutes — First Edition

John Calvin, 27-year-old French exile in Basel, publishes the first edition of Institutes of the Christian Religion — originally a brief catechism, it grows through five editions into the most influential systematic theology of the Reformation. Calvin spent the rest of his life in Geneva, building a pastoral and theological engine that exported Reformed theology across Europe.

1553–1558

Marian Persecution

Mary Tudor attempts to reverse the English Reformation. 300+ Protestants burned at the stake — Cranmer, Latimer, Ridley among them. The Marian exiles flee to Geneva, Frankfurt, and Strasbourg, where they absorb Reformed theology directly. When Elizabeth takes the throne (1558), these exiles return as the theological backbone of English Puritanism.

1563

Heidelberg Catechism

Commissioned by Elector Frederick III for the Palatinate. Written by Zacharias Ursinus and Caspar Olevianus. 129 questions and answers organized around guilt, grace, and gratitude. Becomes the most widely used Reformed catechism worldwide. Its first question — "What is your only comfort in life and in death?" — remains one of the most beloved formulations in Christian history.

1618–1619

Synod of Dort

International synod convened to respond to the Arminian Remonstrance. Produces the Canons of Dort — the famous "Five Points of Calvinism" (TULIP: Total depravity, Unconditional election, Limited atonement, Irresistible grace, Perseverance of the saints). The Canons are not a full theological system — they are a specific response to five specific errors.

1647

Westminster Confession of Faith

The most comprehensive Reformed confession. Produced by the Westminster Assembly (1643–1653) of 121 theologians, convened by the English Parliament. 33 chapters covering Scripture, God, the decrees, creation, providence, the fall, the covenant, Christ, salvation, the law, the church, the sacraments, and the last judgment. Adopted by Presbyterians worldwide. The Baptist version (1689 LBCF) follows its structure closely.

18th Century

The Great Awakening

Jonathan Edwards (1703–1758) — theologian of revival. Religious Affections (1746) distinguishes true gracious affections from natural enthusiasm. George Whitefield (1714–1770) preaches to thousands in open fields across Britain and the American colonies. Reformed theology goes experiential — not just doctrinal precision, but the affections set on God.

19th Century

The Prince of Preachers and Old Princeton

Charles Haddon Spurgeon (1834–1892) — the most famous preacher of the Victorian era, a convinced Calvinist who preached to 10,000 weekly at the Metropolitan Tabernacle. The Hodges (Charles, A.A., and Caspar Wistar) at Princeton Theological Seminary establish Princeton as the intellectual center of American Presbyterian orthodoxy through the 19th and early 20th centuries.

20th Century

Global Spread and Modern Voices

The Reformed tradition globalizes. J. Gresham Machen's stand against liberalism at Princeton leads to Westminster Theological Seminary (1929). The Dutch Reformed tradition produces Abraham Kuyper, Herman Bavinck, and Louis Berkhof. Francis Schaeffer establishes L'Abri. R.C. Sproul founds Ligonier Ministries. John Piper's "Christian Hedonism" brings Edwards into the 21st century. Tim Keller's Redeemer Presbyterian models Reformed ministry in a secular city.

2026

Reformed Theology Today

The tradition spans continents, denominations, and languages. The confessions remain the anchors. The challenges are new — post-Christian secularism, digital media, theological liberalism within and culture-war temptations without — but the task remains what it was in 1517: recover the gospel, preach the Word, administer the sacraments, and trust God to build His church.

Major Reformed Confessions

Heidelberg Catechism

1563 · Palatinate

129 Q&A. Three-part structure: Guilt, Grace, Gratitude. Most pastoral and devotional of the Reformed confessions. Used by Dutch Reformed, German Reformed, and many Presbyterian denominations.

Belgic Confession

1561 · Low Countries

37 articles. Written by Guido de Bres as a defense of Reformed faith to Philip II of Spain. Comprehensive doctrinal statement. One of the Three Forms of Unity.

Canons of Dort

1619 · Netherlands

The Five Points (TULIP). A specific response to five Arminian errors — not a full theological system. Emphasizes God's sovereignty in election and the perseverance of the saints.

Westminster Confession

1647 · England

33 chapters. The most comprehensive Reformed confession. Used by Presbyterians worldwide. Includes Larger and Shorter Catechisms for teaching.

1689 London Baptist

1689 · England

Follows the Westminster structure with Baptist distinctives — believers' baptism and congregational polity. Used by Particular (Calvinistic) Baptists. The most widely used confession among Reformed Baptists globally.

The Solas — What Was Recovered

Sola Scriptura — Scripture alone is the final authority for faith and practice. Not Scripture plus tradition. Not Scripture plus papal decree. The Bible interprets the Bible.
Solus Christus — Christ alone is the mediator. No priest, no saint, no Mary. Salvation is through Christ and Christ only.
Sola Gratia — Grace alone. Salvation is God's work from beginning to end. Not grace plus merit. Not grace plus penance.
Sola Fide — Faith alone as the instrument of justification. Not faith plus works. Faith receives what Christ accomplished.
Soli Deo Gloria — To God alone be the glory. Not to the church. Not to the saints. Not to human free will. The entire economy of salvation exists for God's glory.

Why choose Reformed theology?

Because it takes Scripture most seriously. Because it gives God the most glory. Because it offers the deepest comfort — if salvation depends on God and not on human effort, it is secure. Because it engages the mind without ignoring the affections. Because it has the thickest intellectual tradition, the deepest pastoral resources, and the most global reach of any Protestant tradition.

Reformed theology does not claim to be perfect. It claims to be faithful — to the Word above all, and to the tradition as it faithfully reflects the Word. Where it errs, it must be reformed again. Ecclesia reformata, semper reformanda — the church reformed, always being reformed according to the Word of God.
