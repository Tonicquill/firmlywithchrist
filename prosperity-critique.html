The Most Natural False Religion

Prosperity makes perfect sense as a religion. That's why it's wrong.

It turns the Creator into a vending machine. It makes suffering a sign of failed faith. It promises your best life now. Every instinct in fallen human nature says yes. Every page of Scripture says no.

Why this page exists

The prosperity gospel — the teaching that God wants you healthy, wealthy, and comfortable, that faith unlocks material blessing, that poverty signals spiritual deficiency — is not a fringe distortion. It is the default religion of the human heart. It makes intuitive sense. If God is good and powerful, He should want His people to prosper. If faith pleases Him, He should reward it with visible blessing.

This logic is airtight — on fallen human terms. The Bible dismantles it from Job to Revelation. What follows is not comfortable. It is necessary.

Written hand in hand with the NotebookLM Reformed Theology corpus. Scripture drives the argument. NLM sources provided the historical, confessional, and theological framework.

1. You cannot serve God and Mammon

The biblical text: "No servant can serve two masters; for either he will hate the one and love the other, or else he will be loyal to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and mammon" (Luke 16:13).

Why prosperity "works" as religion: It appeals to unredeemed flesh by making man the master and Christ the servant — a genie obligated to grant health, wealth, and happiness on command. The prosperity preacher says: God wants to bless you. You just need enough faith to unlock it. The transaction is clean. The terms are clear. The customer is always right.

What the Bible actually says: "Those who desire to be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and harmful lusts which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:9–10). Paul does not say wealth is evil. He says the desire for wealth is a trap that drowns people. The prosperity gospel feeds the very desire Scripture warns against and calls it faith.

The hard truth: "Friendship with the world is enmity with God" (James 4:4). The prosperity gospel does not accidentally drift toward worldliness. It baptizes worldliness and calls it blessing. "Your heart will be where your treasure is" (Matthew 6:21). If your treasure is a bigger house, a healed body, a fatter bank account — that is where your heart lives. Not with Christ. With what Christ can give you.

2. Job had everything. Then he had nothing. He worshiped anyway.

The biblical text: "Then Job arose, tore his robe, and shaved his head; and he fell to the ground and worshiped. And he said: 'Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD'" (Job 1:20–21).

Why this dismantles prosperity teaching: If health and wealth signal God's favor, Job's suffering would mean God abandoned him. But the text is explicit: Job's suffering was not punishment. It was a test permitted by God to prove the authenticity of Job's faith. Job's friends arrived with prosperity-gospel logic — "you must have sinned, confess and God will restore you" — and God's verdict at the end of the book is that they spoke wrongly (Job 42:7).

Ecclesiastes weighs in: "He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver; nor he who loves abundance, with increase. This also is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 5:10). The Preacher had more wealth than any prosperity teacher and concluded: all of it is vapor. You accumulate and accumulate, and you die. "As he came from his mother's womb, naked shall he return" (Ecclesiastes 5:15). The prosperity gospel promises satisfaction through accumulation. Ecclesiastes calls that a chasing after wind.

The hard truth: "The LORD makes poor and makes rich; He brings low and lifts up" (1 Samuel 2:7). Both poverty and wealth come from the same hand. Neither is a reliable indicator of spiritual status. The prosperity gospel's central premise — that material conditions map to spiritual reality — is false. It was false for Job. It was false for Lazarus at the rich man's gate. It may be false for you.

3. The prophets: God creates calamity, not just comfort

The biblical text: "I form the light and create darkness, I make peace and create calamity; I, the LORD, do all these things" (Isaiah 45:7). "If there is calamity in a city, will not the LORD have done it?" (Amos 3:6).

Why this shatters the prosperity framework: The prosperity gospel has no category for a God who actively ordains suffering. It must either blame the sufferer (insufficient faith, hidden sin) or blame the devil (spiritual warfare, unbroken curses). The prophets do neither. They declare that God Himself brings catastrophe — and in doing so, He remains perfectly holy, perfectly just, and perfectly worthy of worship.

The prophets' own lives: Jeremiah lived a life of frustration and confusion. He was thrown into a cistern. He was beaten. He was mocked. He never became wealthy. He never saw his ministry "succeed" in visible terms. Yet he never apostatized. The prosperity gospel cannot explain Jeremiah. It would diagnose him as a failure. God called him faithful.

The hard truth: God harnesses the deeds of the wicked and hostile nations to perfect, confirm, strengthen, and establish His people (Isaiah 10:12; 1 Peter 5:10). Suffering is not an interruption of God's plan. It is often the instrument of it. If your theology cannot accommodate a cancer diagnosis, a bankruptcy, or a church of 160 meeting in a borrowed hall with no permit — your theology is too small for the God of the Bible.

4. Jesus: Take up your cross. Lose your life. Expect tribulation.

The biblical text: "If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow Me. For whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake will save it" (Luke 9:23–24). "In the world you will have tribulation. But be of good cheer, I have overcome the world" (John 16:33).

Jesus versus the prosperity teachers: Jesus was homeless. "Foxes have holes and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head" (Luke 9:58). He was tortured to death. His closest followers were martyred. He explicitly said: "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God" (Matthew 19:24). The prosperity gospel's central promise — wealth and comfort now — is the one thing Jesus said makes salvation nearly impossible.

What the cross actually means: "He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32). The "all things" Paul speaks of is not a new car. It is justification, sanctification, glorification — everything necessary for eternal life. If God gave you Christ, you already have the ultimate gift. Demanding a BMW on top of the cross is not faith. It is insult.

The hard truth: The prosperity gospel "omits Jesus and neglects the cross." It replaces genuine fruits of the Spirit with a perverse obsession with this-worldly outcomes. It turns the Savior into a personal trainer and the gospel into a business contract. If your faith would collapse without visible blessing, it is not faith in Christ. It is faith in what Christ can provide. Those are not the same thing.

5. What the Reformed tradition has always taught

The Reformed confessions address prosperity teaching before it had the name. The Westminster Larger Catechism (Q&A 105) lists sins forbidden in the First Commandment, including "making men the lords of our faith and conscience." The prosperity preacher who tells you to name your blessing and claim it by faith is exercising a lordship over your conscience that belongs to God alone.

The Heidelberg Catechism (Q&A 27–28) teaches that God's providence means we can "be patient in adversity, thankful in prosperity." Note the symmetry. Adversity requires patience. Prosperity requires thankfulness. Neither is a judgment. Neither proves anything about the recipient's spiritual state. Both come from the Father's hand.

Calvin, commenting on the rich young ruler, wrote that Christ's command to sell everything was not a universal requirement but a diagnostic — exposing what the man actually worshiped. The prosperity gospel does the reverse. It takes what people actually worship — comfort, health, wealth — and assures them God wants to give them more of it. That is not the gospel. That is idolatry with better branding.

"The firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: 'The Lord knows those who are His,' and, 'Everyone who names the name of the Lord is to abstain from wickedness'" (2 Timothy 2:19).

The god who exists to give you what you want is not God. He is you, projected onto the sky, wearing a Jesus mask. The true God owes you nothing. He already gave you everything that matters — His Son. Everything else is detail.
