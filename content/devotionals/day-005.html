Genesis 3 is the chapter that explains every headline, every broken relationship, every war, every funeral. God had given Adam a single prohibition — one tree in a garden full of trees — and the serpent exploited the gap between God's goodness and God's command. "Did God actually say?" (Genesis 3:1). The serpent's strategy was not to deny God's existence. It was to question God's generosity. The implication: God is holding out on you. The command is not protection; it is restriction. The Creator is not a Father; He is a competitor.

The woman saw that the tree was "good for food," "a delight to the eyes," and "desired to make one wise" (Genesis 3:6). These are the same three categories John identifies as "the desires of the flesh, the desires of the eyes, and the pride of life" (1 John 2:16). The temptation was not to do something obviously evil. It was to reach for something good — wisdom — in a way that defied God's authority. Adam and Eve did not want to stop being like God. They wanted to stop depending on God. The sin was autonomy. "You will be like God" was the serpent's promise. They were already like God — made in His image. The lie was that they needed to seize what they already possessed.

Reformed theology insists on the historicity and the totality of the Fall. Adam was not just the first sinner — he was the federal head of the human race. "In Adam all die" (1 Corinthians 15:22). His sin was imputed to all his descendants. This is the doctrine of original sin, and it is the most empirically verifiable doctrine in the Bible. You do not need to teach a child to lie or to grab or to yell "mine." You have to teach them to share, to tell the truth, to think of others. Something in the human heart is bent. But Genesis 3 is also the first chapter of the gospel. Verse 15 — the protevangelium — promises that the seed of the woman will crush the serpent's head. Judgment and mercy are pronounced in the same breath. The God who asks "Where are you?" already has a rescue plan.
