Genesis 41 records the most dramatic reversal of fortune in the Old Testament. Joseph goes to sleep in Pharaoh's prison. He wakes up as Pharaoh's prime minister — the second most powerful man in Egypt, answerable only to the throne. Pharaoh dresses him in fine linen, puts a gold chain around his neck, gives him a new name (Zaphenath-paneah), and a new wife (Asenath, daughter of an Egyptian priest). In one morning, a Hebrew slave becomes an Egyptian ruler. The speed of the exaltation is breathtaking. But the exaltation was not sudden. It was the culmination of thirteen years — thirteen years since his brothers sold him at seventeen. Thirteen years in pits and slave markets and prisons. Thirteen years of descent. The exaltation took one morning because the preparation took thirteen years.

Pharaoh's dreams — seven fat cows devoured by seven lean cows, seven good ears of grain swallowed by seven thin ears — are interpreted by Joseph as seven years of plenty followed by seven years of famine. Joseph's administrative genius emerges in his proposal: appoint commissioners, collect one-fifth of the harvest during the years of plenty, store the grain, and distribute it during the famine. Egypt is saved. The surrounding nations are saved. The covenant family — Jacob and his sons — will be saved. Joseph's exaltation is not merely a personal vindication. It is a corporate rescue. God raised Joseph up not to punish his brothers but to preserve his brothers. The exaltation of one righteous sufferer becomes the salvation of many.

Philippians 2:8-11 traces the same arc for Christ: "He humbled Himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted Him and bestowed on Him the name that is above every name." The pattern is Christological: descent precedes exaltation, suffering precedes glory, humiliation precedes coronation. Joseph did not save Egypt and his family despite his suffering — he saved them through his suffering. The suffering was not an obstacle to God's plan. It was the mechanism of God's plan. The cross is not a detour. The tomb is not a failure. And the exaltation is certain — not because we are faithful, but because God is. Joseph waited thirteen years. Christ waited three days. Both exaltations were worth the wait.
