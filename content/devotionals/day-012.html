Genesis 12:1-3 is arguably the most important text in the Old Testament after Genesis 1:1. Everything that follows — covenant, exodus, kings, prophets, exile, return, incarnation, cross, resurrection, Great Commission — flows from these three verses. God speaks to Abram, a man from Ur of the Chaldeans, a pagan city at the mouth of the Euphrates. Abram did nothing to earn this call. He was not seeking God. God sought him. The call contains a command and a promise: leave everything, and I will make you into a great nation through whom all the families of the earth will be blessed. The command is costly. The promise is cosmic.

The response is one of the great understatements of Scripture: "So Abram went." Three words in English, three words in Hebrew. No negotiation. No questions. No conditions. Abram does not know where he is going. Hebrews 11:8 says "he went out, not knowing where he was going." This is faith in its purest form: not intellectual assent to a set of propositions, but the willingness to stake your entire life on a promise from a God you have only just met. The Reformed tradition calls this the effectual call — God's word creates the response it commands. God said "go," and Abram went, because God's call carries the power to produce the obedience it demands.

Galatians 3:8 says that God "preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham" when He said "in you shall all the nations be blessed." The promise to Abram was the gospel in seed form. The blessing that would come through Abram's offspring is Christ. The nations blessed through Abram are everyone — Jew and Gentile — who is justified by faith. Paul's argument in Galatians 3 is that the Abrahamic covenant precedes and supersedes the Mosaic law. The promise came first. The law was added "because of transgressions" (Galatians 3:19). The promise is the main event. The call of Abram is the hinge of redemptive history and the pattern of every Christian life: God speaks, we go, and the destination is His to choose.
