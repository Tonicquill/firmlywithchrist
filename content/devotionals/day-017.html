Genesis 22 is the chapter that every reader of Genesis has been dreading. God tells Abraham to take Isaac — the promised son, the son of the covenant, the boy whose name means laughter — and sacrifice him as a burnt offering on Mount Moriah. The text is excruciatingly slow. "Abraham rose early in the morning, saddled his donkey, cut the wood for the burnt offering, and went to the place of which God had told him." Verbs accumulate. Details linger. The journey takes three days. Three days of silence from heaven. Three days to turn back. Abraham does not. Hebrews 11:17-19 tells us what Abraham believed: "God was able even to raise him from the dead." Abraham's faith at Moriah was resurrection faith — the conviction that the God who gave Isaac could give him back again.

The theological center of the chapter is not Abraham's obedience but God's provision. When Isaac asks, "Where is the lamb?" Abraham answers, "God will provide for Himself the lamb." And God does. A ram caught in a thicket — a substitute. Abraham names the place YHWH Yireh, "The LORD will provide." The mountain becomes the geography of substitutionary atonement. The pattern is unmistakable: the son is sentenced, the knife is raised, God intervenes, a substitute dies, and the son lives. This is the gospel in narrative form. Moriah is a preview of Calvary — but at Calvary, there is no ram in the thicket. The Son Himself is the substitute. No voice from heaven stops the execution. The Father provides the Lamb, and the Lamb is His only Son.

James 2:21-23 cites Genesis 22 as evidence that Abraham's faith was genuine because it produced obedience. "Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered up his son Isaac?" Paul and James are not enemies. Paul says works contribute nothing to justification before God. James says works demonstrate justification before men. Abraham was justified in Genesis 15:6 — decades before Moriah. But Moriah proved that Genesis 15:6 was real. Faith alone justifies. But the faith that justifies climbs mountains with a knife and a broken heart, trusting that the God who promised is the God who provides — even when the provision makes no sense until the third day.
