Genesis 15 opens with Abram afraid and doubting. God appears to him in a vision and says, "Fear not, Abram, I am your shield; your reward shall be very great." But Abram is not comforted by abstractions. He wants specifics. He has been promised descendants, but he remains childless. His heir is Eliezer of Damascus, a servant. Abram's question is blunt: "What will You give me, for I continue childless?" God does not rebuke the doubt. He answers it. He takes Abram outside and tells him to count the stars: "So shall your offspring be." Then verse 6, one of the most theologically significant statements in Scripture: "And he believed the LORD, and He counted it to him as righteousness." Abram brought nothing but faith. God credited righteousness to him — the first explicit statement of justification by faith in the Bible.

The covenant ceremony that follows is strange to modern readers and would have been immediately recognizable to Abram. Animals are cut in half and arranged in two rows. In ancient Near Eastern covenant ceremonies, both parties would walk between the pieces, symbolically saying: "If I break this covenant, may what happened to these animals happen to me." But God does something unprecedented. A smoking fire pot and a flaming torch — symbols of God's presence — pass between the pieces alone. Abram does not walk through. The covenant is unilateral. God alone takes the oath. The promise does not depend on Abram's performance. It depends on God's faithfulness. This is the difference between a covenant and a contract: in a contract, both parties perform. In the Abrahamic covenant, God performs, and Abram receives.

This covenant becomes the template for every subsequent covenant in Scripture, culminating in the New Covenant instituted by Christ. "This cup is the new covenant in My blood" (1 Corinthians 11:25). At the Last Supper, Jesus declares that His blood ratifies the covenant — and like Genesis 15, the beneficiary brings nothing but faith. The Westminster Confession (7.1) teaches that God relates to humanity by covenant because "the distance between God and the creature is so great." Covenant bridges that distance. And the bridge rests on God's pillars, not ours.
