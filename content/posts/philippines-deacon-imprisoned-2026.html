On May 28, the Iglesia Filipina Independiente ordained Aldeem Yañez as a deacon inside Lumbia City Jail in the Philippines. He had been detained for four years, arrested in 2022 during a raid his supporters say involved planted weapons and a defective search warrant. His crime, in the eyes of the state, was serving the poor. The Philippine government calls it counter-terrorism. Red-tagging — the practice of labeling clergy and church workers as communist insurgents — has become the standard tool for silencing those who practice pure and undefiled religion before God. Over 700 political prisoners in the Philippines face similar treatment: surveillance, trumped-up charges, and detention for the sin of visiting orphans and widows in their trouble. Yañez was not ordained in a cathedral. He was ordained in a cell. The state believes it has stopped a deacon. It has only demonstrated what true diaconate looks like.[1]

Jesus described the final judgment: "'For I was hungry and you gave Me food; I was thirsty and you gave Me drink; I was a stranger and you took Me in; I was naked and you clothed Me; I was sick and you visited Me; I was in prison and you came to Me'" (Matthew 25:35-36). The Philippine government has read this passage and reversed it. In the Philippines, if you give food to the hungry, the state calls it insurgency. If you give drink to the thirsty, the state calls it subversion. If you visit the prisoner, the state calls you a terrorist and puts you in prison. Aldeem Yañez was not ordaining himself. He was ordaining the state's indictment. The jail cell is not a disqualification from diaconate. It is the qualification. The first deacons in Acts 6 were chosen precisely because they served widows. Yañez has spent four years proving he meets the standard.

James wrote what the Philippine government considers a criminal manifesto: "Pure and undefiled religion before God and the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their trouble, and to keep oneself unspotted from the world" (James 1:27). Pure religion, in the Philippines, gets you red-tagged. Visiting widows gets you surveilled. Keeping oneself unspotted from the world gets you a defective search warrant and planted weapons. The Iglesia Filipina Independiente has not abandoned Yañez. They ordained him. They recognized that the cell does not disqualify the servant — it qualifies him. The Philippine state believes it is protecting national security. It is only protecting its own indifference to the weightier matters of the law. The 700 political prisoners who share Yañez's fate are not a threat to the republic. They are a threat to the republic's conscience.

Paul wrote from prison what Yañez already knows: "For to me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Philippians 1:21). The Philippine state has taken four years from Aldeem Yañez. It cannot take Christ from him. It has locked him in Lumbia City Jail. It cannot lock him out of the kingdom. It has labeled him a terrorist for serving the poor. It cannot label him anything other than what he is: a slave of Jesus Christ, bought at a price, whose life is hidden with Christ in God. To live is Christ — even in a cell. To die is gain — even at the hands of a state that perverts justice. The deacon ordained in jail is not a martyr yet. But he is already a conqueror. Because the state that imprisons the merciful is only imprisoning its own indictment.

The international community will not demand Yañez's release. The human rights organizations will file reports that no one reads. The Philippine government will continue red-tagging clergy while the church continues ordaining deacons in jail cells. And the 700 political prisoners who share his fate will continue being the conscience of a nation that has forgotten what pure religion looks like. But the church remembers. The church remembers Joseph in prison, receiving favor from the keeper because the Lord was with him. The church remembers Paul in chains, writing that his imprisonment advanced the gospel. The church remembers that the Word of God is not chained — not by a defective search warrant, not by planted weapons, not by a cell in Lumbia City Jail. The Philippine state believes it has silenced a deacon. It has only demonstrated that the diaconate was never about cathedrals. It was always about cells. And the God who numbers the hairs on the heads of the imprisoned will by no means clear the guilty. He will repay. And He will do it speedily.
