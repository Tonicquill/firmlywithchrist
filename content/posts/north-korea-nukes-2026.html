May 7, 2026. North Korea amends its constitution to give Kim Jong Un formal, sole authority over the country's nuclear arsenal. The same day, its UN ambassador declares Pyongyang "irreversibly" a nuclear-armed state, not bound by any non-proliferation treaty. A single man now holds constitutional warrant to end millions of lives. The language is legal. The architecture is theological. A mortal has written himself into the position of absolute arbiter of life and death — an office that Scripture reserves for God alone.[1][2]

The world knows what to call this. A geopolitical crisis. A proliferation problem. A diplomatic failure. Scripture calls it something older and more precise: idolatry in constitutional dress. The pattern predates Pyongyang. It shows up in Babel, where men tried to build a tower to the heavens to "make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4). It shows up in Babylon, where Nebuchadnezzar surveyed his kingdom and said, "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built?" (Daniel 4:30). It shows up in the king of Tyre, who said in his heart, "I am a god" (Ezekiel 28:2). Kim Jong Un did not invent this ambition. He inherited it from every ruler who has ever looked at the power to destroy and called it divine right.

Weighed and found wanting

Daniel 5 is the definitive treatment of absolute power under divine judgment. Belshazzar throws a feast, drinks from the temple vessels, and a hand writes on the wall: "MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN" (Daniel 5:25–28). God has numbered your kingdom and put an end to it. You have been weighed in the balances and found wanting. That night Belshazzar was killed, and Darius the Mede took the kingdom.

The handwriting is not on Kim's wall in Pyongyang — not visibly, not yet. But the verdict is already inscribed in the divine court. The Reformed tradition, reflecting on the Westminster Confession of Faith (23.1), teaches that civil magistrates hold only delegated authority. They are "under Him," not beside Him. A constitution does not confer sovereignty. It borrows it, and every breath a ruler takes is borrowed air. Kim Jong Un can amend the North Korean constitution as many times as he likes. He cannot amend the decree of the Most High: "The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up" (1 Samuel 2:6–7). One throne holds the monopoly on life and death. It was occupied before Kim was born and will be occupied after his kingdom is dust.

The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together, against the LORD and against His Anointed. He who sits in the heavens shall laugh.

Psalm 2:2,4

Isaiah 14 names the ambition Kim's constitutional amendment embodies: "I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God; I will also sit on the mount of the congregation on the farthest sides of the north; I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will be like the Most High" (Isaiah 14:13–15). The five "I wills" are the architecture of idolatrous power. Each one climbs higher. Each one claims more. The fall, when it comes, is from the highest point to the lowest depth.

Psalm 2 gives the divine response: "He who sits in the heavens shall laugh; the LORD shall hold them in derision" (Psalm 2:2–5). The Hebrew verb for "laugh" is yishaq — not amused laughter but sovereign derision. This is not God finding Kim funny. This is God finding Kim futile. The man who claims the power to incinerate cities is, from heaven's perspective, a man whose finger is on a button God installed.

A constitution does not confer sovereignty. It borrows it. Kim Jong Un can amend every document in Pyongyang. He cannot amend the verdict: weighed and found wanting.
