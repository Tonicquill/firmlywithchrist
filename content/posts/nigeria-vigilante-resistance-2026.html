At least thirty-four Christians were killed in Kogi and Plateau states in early May. Muslim Fulani herdsmen descended on villages, burned homes, and slaughtered whoever could not run. Among the dead was a police officer — the very figure Romans 13 calls God's minister to bear the sword against evil. The state was present. The state died. And in other attacks, in Kaduna and Plateau, vigilantes who received advance warning mounted armed resistance and drove the raiders back.[1] The question is not whether force is ever justified. The question is who holds the sword when the state drops it.

Nehemiah stood on the wall of Jerusalem and saw the same thing: an enemy approaching, workers exposed, and no Persian garrison in sight. His response was not pacifist resignation. It was a call to arms: "Do not be afraid of them. Remember the Lord, great and awesome, and fight for your brethren, your sons, your daughters, your wives, and your houses" (Nehemiah 4:14). The Reformed tradition has always held that lawful defense of life and property is nowhere forbidden in Scripture. What is forbidden is the church wielding the sword for the expansion of its influence — a confusion that produced the Crusades and Cromwell's miseries. But a farmer defending his family from a midnight raid is not a crusader. He is a steward.

Nehemiah 4:14

Jesus said, "Whoever slaps you on your right cheek, turn the other to him also" (Matthew 5:38-39). The Reformed tradition, following MacArthur and the historic expositors, insists this text addresses personal insults and persecution for Christ's sake — not criminal assault. To apply "turn the other cheek" to a man murdering your neighbor is not spiritual maturity. It is moral abdication. The same Jesus who spoke those words also drove money-changers from the temple with a whip. The same Savior who submitted to the cross warned His disciples to sell their cloaks and buy swords. Context is not a loophole. It is the difference between martyrdom and suicide.

Romans 13:4 calls the civil magistrate "God's minister to you for good... an avenger to execute wrath on him who practices evil." The Nigerian state has failed this mandate. A new report accuses the government of a $10-million cover-up to hide the scale of Christian genocide — 190,000 killed since 2009, an estimated 70% of them Christians, while officials mischaracterize the violence as regional instability or climate change.[2] When the minister appointed by God refuses to bear the sword, the duty of protection does not evaporate. It disperses. It falls to fathers, to neighbors, to the community that Nehemiah armed with swords and trowels — building with one hand, defending with the other.

The vigilantes who resisted in Kaduna and Plateau did not launch a holy war. They received intelligence, prepared a defense, and protected their villages. They did what the state would not. This is not vengeance. Vengeance belongs to the Lord. This is stewardship — the same stewardship that says a man who does not provide for his own household has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever. The Nigerian Christian who picks up a rifle to stop a machete is not violating the Sermon on the Mount. He is fulfilling it — by loving his neighbor as himself, with whatever means the moment demands.

Thirty-four Christians died in Nigeria because the state meant to protect them failed. In other villages, vigilantes armed themselves and lived. The sword belongs to God. He lends it to the magistrate. When the magistrate drops it, the steward picks it up.
