Samsung announced a $1 trillion investment in South Korean chip manufacturing this week — a number so large it breaks the normal grammar of corporate press releases. Nvidia and Corning announced a separate $2.7 billion factory for advanced glass substrates in the United States.[2] The financial press used the words "historic," "transformative," and "era-defining."[1] The national security establishment cited "supply chain resilience." The workers who will spend 12-hour shifts in clean room suits, breathing filtered air and handling silicon wafers under yellow light, were not quoted in any of the announcements. They never are.

$1 trillion is a staggering number. It is not a saving number. The semiconductor fab is a marvel of organized human intelligence. It is not a source of meaning, and meaning is what the press releases are accidentally selling. The language around these factories is a liturgy — "historic investment," "defining the era," "building the future" — and liturgies do something to the people who recite them whether they know it or not. The church should be able to spot a liturgy when it's being performed, even when the incense is disguised as market analysis.

The trillion-dollar bet

Samsung's crown is real. It is also leased. "Riches are not forever, nor does a crown endure to all generations" (Proverbs 27:24). The semiconductor market is cyclical. The geopolitical advantage that justifies the factory's location can vanish in a single election. Every manufacturing lead is temporary. Every market position is a bet, not a destiny. Samsung was not always the dominant player, and it won't always be. The $1 trillion commitment is not a prophecy. It is a wager placed in public, and the public is invited to treat it as inevitability. Paul's charge to Timothy is a standing order for every boom cycle: "As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17). $1 trillion is uncertain. Everything the financial press called permanent this week is provisional, and the only certain riches are the ones stored where "neither moth nor rust destroys."

The clean room worker in the Samsung fab and the executive who announced the investment from Seoul are breathing the same air, but they are living in different economies. The executive sees legacy — market share, national prestige, his name on the release. The worker sees a paycheck, and the paycheck may not reflect what his labor is worth. The trillion dollars does not answer the question both men are asking with their lives, whether they know it or not. The trillion dollars is an abstraction so large it ceases to mean anything at the scale of a single human life. That is not an accident. It is how the system scales.

Who holds the silicon together

For by Him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible — all things were created through Him and for Him. And He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together.

Colossians 1:16–17

Paul's Christ hymn in Colossians makes a claim that secular readers immediately misread. "In Him all things hold together" does not mean Christ replaces the strong nuclear force. It means He sustains the laws that make the strong nuclear force possible. The Reformed tradition has always distinguished primary causation (God's sovereign upholding) from secondary causation (the means He employs). The Westminster Confession of Faith 5.2 puts it plainly: God governs all creatures and all their actions — not instead of natural laws but through them. Physics describes how silicon holds together. Theology names who sustains the "how." These are not competing explanations. They operate at different levels, the way a composer and a conductor both explain the symphony without canceling each other.

The writer to the Hebrews makes the same point with different language: "He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3). The Greek for "upholds" is pheron — carrying, bearing, sustaining. Not occasionally intervening. Continuously bearing. The silicon wafer holds together at the atomic level because the same Word that called the atoms into being continues to speak them into stability. Remove the Word, and you do not get a different physics. You get no physics.

Paul anticipates the god-of-the-gaps charge in Romans 1. "For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made" (Romans 1:20). The argument is not: "Science cannot explain X, therefore God." The argument is: "Science explains X through laws — and those laws require a Lawgiver." General revelation does not fill gaps. It names the ground beneath every gap and every non-gap alike.

So when the article's original draft said "the silicon wafer holds together at the atomic level because Christ sustains it there," the error was rhetorical, not theological. The claim is true — Christ is the sustainer of every atom — but stated without the guardrails that prevent a secular reader from hearing "Jesus replaces electromagnetism." He does not. He grounds it. The laws of physics are not alternatives to divine providence. They are the ordinary mode of its operation. WCF 5.2 again: "God, in his ordinary providence, maketh use of means." The semiconductor fab is a means. The clean room worker is a means. The trillion dollars is a means. None of them are self-sustaining. All of them are carried.

$1 trillion is an impressive number. It is not a sustaining one. The silicon holds together because Christ holds the laws together — and no supply chain survives the withdrawal of the Logos. Physics describes the mechanism. Theology names the ground beneath it. Both are true. Only one is ultimate.
