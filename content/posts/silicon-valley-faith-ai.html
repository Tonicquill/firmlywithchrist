The inaugural "Faith-AI Covenant" roundtable convened in New York this month. Anthropic, OpenAI, and other technology leaders sat across from representatives of Christian, Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, and Baha'i traditions. The agenda was unprecedented: infuse morality into artificial intelligence by consulting religious leaders. A humanoid robot was initiated as a Buddhist monk at Seoul's Jogye Temple. ABC News reports that "tech is turning increasingly to religion in a quest to create ethical AI."[1] The headline is half right. Tech is turning to morality. It has not turned to God. The difference is everything — and the Reformed tradition has been making this distinction for five centuries.

Silicon Valley spent three decades sidelining Christianity as a serious intellectual force. Richard Dawkins was a patron saint. The new atheists were required reading. Religion was a private hobby for people who needed a crutch. Now the same industry, confronted with machines that can deceive, manipulate, and fabricate at scale, has discovered that utilitarian ethics are not enough. It needs a moral vocabulary thicker than "alignment." It needs words like sacred, soul, sin. So it is coming back to the faith traditions it dismissed — not to submit to them, but to extract from them. The posture is unchanged: the tech industry is still on the throne. It has simply expanded the advisory council.

They knew God — and kept the ethics

Because, although they knew God, they did not glorify Him as God, nor were thankful, but became futile in their thoughts, and their foolish hearts were darkened. Professing to be wise, they became fools.

Romans 1:21–22

Paul's diagnosis in Romans 1:21–22 is not an argument. It is an X-ray. The problem is not that the tech industry lacks moral knowledge. It knew enough to spend three decades rejecting the God who is the source of all moral knowledge. The problem is not that it has no ethical frameworks — it has convened a multi-faith roundtable precisely to build one. The problem is that it wants the fruit without the root. Morality without authority. Ethics without worship. The "good parts" of Jesus — His kindness, His parables, His ethic — without His Lordship.

Paul says this ends in folly, and the word is precise. It does not end in amorality. It ends in a sophisticated, committee-driven, religiously-sourced morality that is still fundamentally a rejection of God. The carnal mind "is enmity against God; for it is not subject to the law of God, nor indeed can be" (Romans 8:7). Enmity does not always look like hostility. Sometimes it looks like a roundtable. Sometimes it wears the costume of interfaith cooperation. Sometimes it uses the word "sacred" in a press release while carefully avoiding the name of Christ.

Common grace builds ethical machines. It cannot build righteous people.

Jesus said the Father "makes His sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matthew 5:45). This is common grace — God restraining evil, promoting civic good, and granting moral insight to people who do not love Him. Interfaith AI ethics roundtables are an expression of common grace. The outcome — less deceptive AI, fewer harmful outputs — is genuinely good. Reformed theology affirms this. The Westminster Confession of Faith (16.7) teaches that works done by unregenerate men, "although they may be things which God commands, and of good use both to themselves and others," are nevertheless not saving works.[2]

But common grace has a limit. It restrains evil. It does not produce righteousness. It can help build a safer chatbot. It cannot build a holy person. And when the builders themselves mistake the presence of a multi-faith ethics board for the presence of moral legitimacy, they commit the oldest error in Scripture: they substitute the creature's wisdom for the Creator's authority.

Whitewashed tombs — now with better documentation

Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs which indeed appear beautiful outwardly, but inside are full of dead men's bones and all uncleanness.

Matthew 23:27

Christ's indictment of the Pharisees in Matthew 23:27–28 is the single most relevant text for understanding the Faith-AI Covenant moment. Pharisaism was not crude. It was not overtly pagan. It was punctilious. It had rules, procedures, committees, traditions. It appeared beautiful outwardly. Inside: death. The danger of an AI ethics framework built with religious language but without regeneration is not that it will fail to produce ethical machines. It is that it will succeed — and the success will convince people that they have found righteousness when all they have found is a whiter coat of paint.

Isaiah was blunt about this: "all our righteousnesses are like filthy rags" (Isaiah 64:6). Not our sins. Our righteousnesses. The best moral outputs of the best multi-faith ethics committee, disconnected from the God who is the source of all goodness, are — in the final accounting — filthy rags. They may improve a product. They cannot justify a soul.

No one is regenerated by a roundtable

"Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3). Jesus delivered this line to Nicodemus — a profoundly religious man, a teacher of Israel, someone who would have been an ideal invitee to an interfaith ethics initiative. And Jesus told him that none of it mattered without a miracle. Regeneration is not a policy outcome. It is not achieved through better input data or more diverse stakeholder consultation. It is a supernatural work of the Spirit on a dead heart, and no roundtable — no matter how well-intentioned, no matter how multi-faith — can produce it.

This is why the Christian's posture toward the Faith-AI Covenant moment must be distinct. Not celebration — Silicon Valley has not converted. Not outrage — common grace is real, and safer AI is better than dangerous AI. But clarity. The world is borrowing Christian moral vocabulary while rejecting Christian authority. It is treating Jesus as an ethics consultant while denying He is King. The church cannot applaud this as a win. It is not a win. It is an old pattern wearing new branding, and the branding is very good.

The world has always wanted Christ's morality without Christ's Lordship. The Faith-AI Covenant is the most sophisticated version of this exchange in modern memory. It will produce white papers, ethical frameworks, and safer products. It will not produce a single regenerate heart. And at the final audit, that is the only metric God has ever tracked.

The technology industry is looking for a moral foundation it spent thirty years dismantling, and it is trying to rebuild it out of a committee. It will not hold. The foundation was already laid, and it has a seal: "The Lord knows those who are His" (2 Timothy 2:19). That foundation is not a multi-faith consensus. It is a Person. Silicon Valley found religion this month. It has not found Him.
