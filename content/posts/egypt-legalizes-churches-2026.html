Egypt approved legal status for 191 more churches this week — the thirtieth batch under the 2016 Law for Building and Restoring Churches. The number is not the headline. The headline is the verb. Approved. As though the church of Jesus Christ were a zoning application. As though the body for which the Son of God shed His own blood needed a building permit from Cairo to exist.[1] The Egyptian government did something genuinely good. It removed a legal obstacle. Christians in Minya and Assiut and Sohag will gather in buildings that the state now recognizes. That is worth acknowledging. But a Christian who understands who builds the church will also recognize that the state did nothing more than stop claiming a jurisdiction it never possessed. Christ authorized the church two thousand years before Egypt's parliament existed.

This is not a news-reactive hot take. It is a theological explainer, because the question beneath the headline is one every believer must answer: who authorizes the church to exist and gather? The state? The culture? The pastor? The denomination? Or Christ alone? Scripture's answer is not ambiguous. And in 2026, when governments from Cairo to Kazan to Chhattisgarh are claiming the right to permit or prohibit Christian worship, knowing the answer is not an academic exercise. It is a confessional necessity.

Christ builds. The state does not.

And I also say to you that you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it.

Matthew 16:18

Jesus does not say, "The apostles will build My church." He does not say, "The Roman Empire will permit My church." He does not say, "The Department of Religious Affairs will register My church." He says I will build My church. The subject is Christ. The object is His church. The promise is absolute: the gates of Hades — the power of death itself — will not withstand what He builds. A government permit is thinner than the gates of Hades. If death cannot stop the church, a bureaucrat cannot either.

Paul unpacks the architecture: "He put all things under His feet, and gave Him to be head over all things to the church, which is His body, the fullness of Him who fills all in all" (Ephesians 1:22–23). Christ is not the church's figurehead. He is its Head — the one from whom the body receives life, direction, and continued existence. No parliament, president, or prime minister occupies that office. The Reformed tradition expresses this in Westminster Confession of Faith 25.1: the church is "gathered and perfected" by Christ alone. Not registered. Not permitted. Not approved. Gathered. The verb belongs to the Head.

What Caesar gets, and what he doesn't

"Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Matthew 22:21). Christ draws a boundary here that no government has any right to erase. Caesar gets taxes, civil order, the administration of temporal justice. Caesar does not get the worship of the living God. Caesar does not get jurisdiction over the Word preached, the sacraments administered, or the prayers of the saints. When Egypt issues a permit to a church, it operates within its God-given sphere — maintaining civil order by recognizing a legal entity. When Egypt suggests the church needs that permit to be the church, it has crossed Christ's boundary.

This is the two-kingdoms doctrine that runs through the Reformed confessions. Westminster Confession 23.3 declares that the civil magistrate may not "assume to himself the administration of the Word and sacraments, or the power of the keys of the kingdom of heaven." The state's authority is real — but it is bounded. It governs the body. It does not govern the soul. It can register a building. It cannot authorize a church. The distinction is not academic. Christians have died for it.

We ought to obey God rather than men.

Acts 5:29

The pattern that runs the whole story

The question of who authorizes the church is not a New Testament novelty. It runs from Genesis to Revelation. Pharaoh told the Hebrew midwives to kill the male children; they "feared God, and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them" (Exodus 1:17). Daniel knew the king's edict forbade prayer to anyone but the king; "he went home. And in his upper room, with his windows open toward Jerusalem, he knelt down on his knees three times that day" (Daniel 6:10). The apostles were commanded by the Sanhedrin — the highest religious and civil court in Israel — to stop preaching. Their reply is the irreducible principle of Christian political theology: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).

Notice the pattern. In every case, the state's command was legally valid. The king's edict was on the books. The Sanhedrin's prohibition carried the force of law. Pharaoh's decree was official policy. But a legally valid command can be theologically void. The Reformed tradition has always known this. John Calvin, commenting on Acts 5:29, writes that "in obeying God, we must not think it much to displease men." The church's existence does not rest on statutory authorization, and therefore no statutory prohibition can end it.

The church of Jesus Christ is the only institution in the world whose existence was authorized by a single act — the shed blood of the Son of God — and no subsequent act of any government can either validate it or revoke it.

The only kingdom that stands

The final frame is not a building permit. It is a coronation. "The God of heaven will set up a kingdom which shall never be destroyed; and the kingdom shall not be left to other people; it shall break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms, and it shall stand forever" (Daniel 2:44). Every government that now claims the right to permit or prohibit Christian worship will be swept away by that kingdom. Egypt's parliament. Russia's Duma. The Sanhedrin that condemned the apostles no longer exists. The Roman Empire that threw Christians to lions is an archaeological site. "The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ, and He shall reign forever and ever" (Revelation 11:15).

Egypt legalizing 191 churches is good news. Let it be received as such. Christians who can now gather without fear should give thanks. But let no one confuse a building permit with an authorization. Christ authorized the church at Calvary. He commissioned it at the Great Commission (Matthew 28:18–20). He will present it to Himself in glory, "a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:27). No government participates in any of those acts. The church does not need the state's permission to be who she already is. The foundation holds its seal: "The Lord knows those who are His" (2 Timothy 2:19).
