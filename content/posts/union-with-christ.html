Ask the average Christian how they are saved, and you will hear a list: forgiven, justified, adopted, sanctified, glorified. Ask the average Christian what connects those blessings to their actual life, and the answer gets quieter. The Reformed tradition has a single answer, and it is so central that the Westminster Confession of Faith dedicates an entire chapter to it: union with Christ. Not a metaphor. Not a feeling. A real, spiritual, personal bond that makes every other blessing possible.

John Calvin called union with Christ "the highest degree of salvation" — not because it is one blessing among many, but because it is the channel through which all blessings flow. You are not forgiven and then joined to Christ. You are forgiven because you are joined to Christ. You are not sanctified and then adopted. You are sanctified and adopted because you are in Him. The order is not sequential. It is organic. The branch does not receive sap, sunlight, and stability from three separate sources. It receives everything from the vine.

John 15:4-5 is the most direct statement of union in the New Testament. Jesus does not say, "Learn from me," or "Follow my example," or even "Believe my teachings." He says, "Abide in me." The Greek word menō means to remain, to dwell, to stay. It is the same word used in John 1:14 for the Word "dwelling" among us. Union with Christ is the continuation of the Incarnation — not in the flesh, but in the Spirit. The same Spirit who conceived Christ in Mary's womb now conceives Christ in the believer's heart. That is why Paul can say, Galatians 2:20. He is not speaking poetically. He is describing a real, personal indwelling.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, chapter 26, calls this "that mystical union" which exists between Christ and believers. It is mystical not because it is vague, but because it is spiritual — effected by the Holy Spirit, not by human will or human merit. The Spirit applies to the individual what Christ accomplished on the cross. Justification, adoption, sanctification, and glorification are not separate packages the believer picks up at different counters. They are different aspects of one reality: the believer is in Christ. That is why Paul uses the phrase en Christō — "in Christ" — roughly 160 times. It is not rhetorical padding. It is the gravitational center of Pauline theology.

And because of him you are in Christ Jesus, who became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption.

1 Corinthians 1:30

The pastoral implication is severe and comforting. Severe, because it means you cannot have some of Christ. You cannot have His forgiveness without His lordship, His peace without His Spirit, His heaven without His holiness. The branch does not negotiate with the vine. Comforting, because it means you cannot lose some of Christ either. If you are in Him, you are in Him wholly — justified, adopted, indwelt, sealed, and kept. Colossians 3:3-4 says your life is hidden with Christ in God. Hidden means secure. It means the world cannot see it, the devil cannot reach it, and death cannot end it. It is tucked inside the life of the Son, and the Son is tucked inside the Father. The believer is in Christ, Christ is in the Father, and the Spirit is in the believer — a trinitarian nesting doll of security.

Theologian John Murray, whose Redemption Accomplished and Applied remains a standard text in Reformed seminaries, argued that union with Christ is the "central truth of the whole doctrine of salvation." He was not overstating. Without union, justification is a legal fiction — God declaring righteous someone who is still a stranger. Without union, sanctification is moralism — a sinner trying to improve himself without a new nature. Without union, adoption is sentiment — God feeling fond of someone He does not know. Union makes every blessing real because union makes the believer really connected to the One in whom every blessing exists.

The application is not technique. You do not achieve union through a method, a prayer, or a spiritual discipline. You receive it through faith, and you experience it through the ordinary means of grace: the preached Word, the sacraments, and prayer. The branch does not work at remaining in the vine. It simply remains. Abiding is not striving. It is staying. The striving belongs to the Vine-keeper, who prunes, protects, and feeds. The branch's job is to receive. That is not passivity. It is the most active dependence there is.

You are not forgiven and then joined to Christ. You are forgiven because you are joined to Christ. Every blessing flows from one source: the vine.
