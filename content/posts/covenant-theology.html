Open the Bible to Genesis 1, and you meet a Creator who speaks worlds into existence. Turn to Revelation 22, and you meet a Lamb on a throne, a tree of life, and a city where death no longer exists. Between those two pages lie sixty-five more books, forty different authors, three languages, two testaments, and roughly fifteen hundred years of composition. Most readers treat it like an anthology — a collection of wisdom sayings, battle stories, poems, and prophecies that happen to share a binding. The Reformed tradition has never read it that way.

Covenant theology is the claim that the Bible is not an anthology. It is a single, continuous narrative held together by one structural device: the covenant. Not a contract. Not a treaty. A covenant — a bond of promise, oath, and grace that binds God to His people across every generation, every empire, and every literary genre. The Reformers did not invent this reading. They inherited it from Augustine, refined it against the Anabaptists, and crystallized it in the Westminster Confession of Faith, chapter 7. But the covenant is not a Reformed innovation. It is the Bible's own grammar.

Genesis 3:15 is the first covenant promise. After the fall, God does not abandon Adam and Eve to self-improvement. He announces a rescue: the seed of the woman will crush the serpent's head. That promise shapes everything that follows. Noah receives it through water. Abraham receives it through circumcision and land. Moses receives it through law and sacrifice. David receives it through dynasty. And the prophets keep insisting that the covenant is not broken — it is remembered. Jeremiah announces a new covenant written on hearts, not stone. Ezekiel announces a new heart and a new spirit. Both prophets are doing the same thing: they are not replacing the old covenant with something unrelated. They are promising its fulfillment.

The Westminster Confession of Faith distinguishes two covenants: the covenant of works and the covenant of grace. The first was made with Adam in innocence: obey and live, disobey and die. Adam disobeyed. The second was made with Christ as the federal head of His people: believe and live, because Christ obeyed and died in your place. Every human being is in one of these two covenants. There is no neutral ground. There is no third option. The covenant of works still operates for those outside Christ — they are under its curse. The covenant of grace operates for those in Christ — they are under its blessing. The entire Bible is the story of these two covenants colliding, with the second swallowing the first at Calvary.

Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah... I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts.

Jeremiah 31:31-33

What this means for reading: when you see Moses, you are not reading ancient Israelite legislation. You are reading the administration of the same covenant of grace that Paul preaches in Romans, wrapped in types, shadows, and national form. The sacrifices pointed to Christ. The land pointed to heaven. The priesthood pointed to the one Mediator. The law pointed to the Spirit's work of sanctification. The old covenant was not a failed experiment. It was a pedagogue — a tutor leading its students to the schoolmaster who is Christ. Galatians 3:23-24 says the law was a guardian until faith came. A guardian is not an enemy. A guardian is temporary protection for minors who are not yet ready for full inheritance.

Herman Bavinck, the Dutch theologian whose Reformed Dogmatics remains the most comprehensive systematic theology of the tradition, argued that covenant theology protects the unity of Scripture against two opposite errors. The first error is dispensationalism — the view that God has radically different plans for Israel and the church, as if the Bible contained two separate religions. The second error is liberalism — the view that the Old Testament is primitive and the New Testament is advanced, as if progress meant leaving Moses behind. Covenant theology says neither. It says Moses and Paul are teaching the same religion, the same God, the same grace, the same redemption — one in shadow, the other in substance.

The practical payoff is coherence. A Christian who reads the Bible covenantally does not wonder why God seems so angry in Exodus and so tender in John. The anger is against covenant breakers. The tenderness is toward covenant keepers — those who are kept not by their own performance but by the performance of Christ, imputed to them through faith. Every command in Scripture is either a revelation of what God requires (and what Christ fulfilled) or a revelation of what God enables (and what the Spirit works). The Bible is not a maze of disconnected moralisms. It is the autobiography of a covenant-keeping God who does not abandon His promises.

The Bible is not sixty-six books that happen to share a binding. It is one book, one covenant, one rescue, told in two covenants — works and grace — with the second swallowing the first at Calvary.
