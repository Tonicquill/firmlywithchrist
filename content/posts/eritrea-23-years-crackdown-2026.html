On May 21, Christian advocacy groups gathered outside the Eritrean Embassy in London to mark twenty-three years since the government outlawed every religious group outside state-sanctioned churches and Sunni Islam. Twenty-three years. That is not a season of trial. It is a generation. And one believer has spent eight of those years in solitary confinement inside a shipping container. The government did not forget him. It simply decided he was not worth releasing.[1]

Joseph was thrown into a pit by his brothers, sold to slavers, and later imprisoned in Egypt on a false charge. The text does not say he was released quickly. It says the Lord was with him. Genesis 39:21 does not promise deliverance from the cell. It promises presence in it. For the Eritrean Christian in a shipping container, this is not theology for a seminar. It is the only hope that does not rot. The same God who superintended Joseph's dungeon superintends every shipping container, every unmarked grave, every cell where the state believes it has achieved invisibility.

But the LORD was with Joseph and showed him mercy, and He gave him favor in the sight of the keeper of the prison.

Genesis 39:21

Revelation 6:10 records the cry of the martyrs under the altar: "How long, O Lord, holy and true, until You judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" The question is not a failure of faith. It is the voice of faith under prolonged duress. The apparent delay of relief is the measure of God's longsuffering, but it is not indefinite. Revelation 6:10 teaches that God hears the cry of the persecuted. He does not slumber. He does not forget. The prisoner in the shipping container has a name, and the Lord knows it — even if the embassy protestors do not.

Paul wrote from a Roman prison: "For to me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Philippians 1:21). This is not stoic resignation. It is the calculus of a man who has already died with Christ and knows that neither death nor life, neither principalities nor powers, neither shipping containers nor solitary confinement, can separate him from the love of God. The Eritrean church has endured twenty-three years of systematic suppression. Pastors have died in custody. Believers have been held in metal shipping containers under the desert sun. And yet the church has not been eradicated. It has been refined. The same sovereignty that permitted the crackdown sustains the saints through it.

The world will forget this vigil by next week. The embassy protestors will go home. The news cycle will move on. But the prisoner in the container does not need the world's memory. He needs the Lord's presence — and that presence is not subject to diplomatic pressure or public awareness. It is promised. Revelation 21:4 is not a comfort for the comfortable. It is a verdict on every regime that has tried to write itself into God's office. The Day of the Lord will bring swift and complete justice. The shipping container will rust. The prisoner will rise. And the God who heard Joseph in Egypt will hear him in Eritrea.

Twenty-three years of crackdown. Eight years in a shipping container. The Eritrean state believes time is on its side. It is not. The Lord was with Joseph in the dungeon. He is with the prisoner in the container. And He will be there when the door finally opens — or when the prisoner walks through a better door.
