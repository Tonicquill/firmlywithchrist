Forty-six Christians were kidnapped in southwestern Nigeria this week. Their ages range from two to sixteen. The attackers struck three Baptist schools in the Oyo region — Yawota Nursery and Primary School and two facilities in Esiele — in a coordinated assault that emptied classrooms and emptied headlines. The Christian Association of Nigeria confirmed the abductions. Classes were suspended. They resumed Monday. The children have not returned. The world scrolled past.[1]

The Bible has a word for this moment, and the word is pit. Joseph's brothers threw him into one. They sold him to Midianite traders for twenty shekels of silver. He was seventeen. The text does not say God was absent from the pit. It says the pit was where God began the work that would save a nation from famine. Years later, Joseph looked his brothers in the eye and said what no human strategy could have produced: "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive" (Genesis 50:20). The kidnappers in Oyo meant evil. God has not promised to explain how He will mean it for good. He has promised that He will. That promise is not a distraction from the horror. It is the only thing that makes the horror endurable.

The second word the Bible speaks into this silence is see. Moses was herding sheep in Midian when God appeared in a burning bush and said: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings, and I have come down to deliver them" (Exodus 3:7-8). Egypt did not see. Pharaoh heard the cries and hardened his heart. The taskmasters heard and increased the quota. The nations of the ancient Near East heard and did nothing. God's response was not to join their indifference. It was to come down. The God who sees affliction when the world scrolls past is the same God who delivers when the world has moved on to the next headline. The children in Oyo have a witness that CNN does not.

He who shuts his ear to the cry of the poor will also cry himself and not be answered.

Proverbs 21:13

But the Bible does not only comfort the afflicted. It afflicts the comfortable. Jesus told a story about a man beaten and left half-dead on the road to Jericho. A priest saw him and passed by on the other side. A Levite saw him and passed by on the other side. They were not cruel men. They were busy men. They had appointments. They had reputations. They had reasons. And Jesus made them the villains of the story. "Whoever shuts his ear to the cry of the poor will also cry himself and not be answered" (Proverbs 21:13). The indifference of the priest was not a neutral choice. It was a moral failure that Jesus held up for eternal contempt. The scroll is a choice. The skip is a sin. The Christian who reads about forty-six kidnapped children and moves on to sports scores is not committing a small negligence. He is auditioning for the role of the priest.

The third word is vengeance. Deuteronomy 32 records God's response to violence against His people: "Vengeance is mine, and recompense... See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god beside me; I kill and I make alive" (Deuteronomy 32:35, 39). God does not outsource vengeance to the United Nations. He does not delegate it to hashtags. He keeps it — literally, in Hebrew, to me belongs vengeance — because only the omniscient Judge can execute it without becoming the next perpetrator. The kidnappers in Oyo will answer for what they have done. Not eventually. Not maybe. Certainly. The God who says "I kill and I make alive" is the same God who says "I wound and I heal." He is not safe. He is good. And His goodness includes the certainty that no violence against His image-bearers goes unanswered.

The final word is finish. Paul wrote to Philippi: "I am sure of this, that he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6). The children who were taken are not abandoned projects. They are image-bearers in whom God began a good work. He will finish it. The parents who sent them to school on Monday morning and have not seen them since are not praying to a God who started something He cannot complete. The God who sees, who comes down, who avenges, is the God who finishes. The day of Jesus Christ is the horizon of every kidnapped child, every grieving parent, every unanswered prayer. The completion is not conditional on rescue. It is conditional on God's faithfulness, which is the only condition in the universe that has never been broken.

The kidnappers in Oyo meant evil. God meant it for good. The world scrolled past. God came down. Vengeance belongs to Him. And He who began a good work in every one of those children will bring it to completion — at the day of Jesus Christ, if not before.
