The Pharisee Is in Your Mirror

Self-righteousness is the sin you never notice. Jesus said more about it than almost any other — and modern believers assume it applies to someone else. What the log in your eye actually means, why the Pharisee prayed to himself, and how the tax collector went home justified while the theologian did not.

Bible Lessons Global Matthew 7:1-5 Luke 18:9-14 Jeremiah 17:9 Romans 2:3-4 1 John 1:8-9 Galatians 6:4

May 2026. A celebrity pastor falls. A politician's old tweets surface. A CEO donates millions and is exposed for labor violations in the same week. The pattern is familiar. The public rushes to judgment. The internet distributes verdicts before evidence arrives. And everyone participating feels righteous doing it. Here is what Jesus said about that feeling.

Self-righteousness is not a sin the self-righteous notice. That is the design. The Pharisee does not wake up and decide to perform holiness. He wakes up convinced he is holy. The performance is invisible to the performer. This is why Jesus spoke about it more than almost any other sin — and why modern believers assume it applies to someone else.

The Log in Your Eye

Jesus did not say "judge not." He said "Judge not, that you be not judged. For with what judgment you judge, you will be judged; and with the measure you use, it will be measured back to you. And why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye, but do not consider the plank in your own eye? Or how can you say to your brother, 'Let me remove the speck from your eye'; and look, a plank is in your own eye? Hypocrite! First remove the plank from your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye" (Matthew 7:1–5).

The Pharisee's error was not that he saw the speck. It was that he could not see the plank. His vision worked perfectly on others and not at all on himself. The modern equivalent is not hard to find. The Christian who spots heresy in every sermon but never examines his own marriage. The commenter who detects moral failure in every public figure but never confesses his own private sin. The church member who knows exactly what is wrong with every other congregation but treats his own as above reproach.

Jesus calls this person a hypocrite — hypocrites — the same word he uses for the Pharisees in Matthew 23. The moral diagnostician who cannot diagnose himself is not a separate category from the Pharisee. He is the Pharisee. And the Pharisee, Jesus said elsewhere, is like "a whitewashed tomb which indeed appears beautiful outwardly, but inside is full of dead men's bones and all uncleanness" (Matthew 23:27–28). The outside is polished. The inside is death. And the occupant does not know the difference.

The Temple and the Tax Collector

Luke records the parable Jesus told "to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others" (Luke 18:9). The audience is the key. He did not tell this to tax collectors. He told it to people who were certain of their own righteousness — which means he told it to church people. The people who show up, who know the Bible, who do not cheat or commit obvious scandal. Those people.

"The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, 'God, I thank You that I am not like other men — extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this tax collector. I fast twice a week; I give tithes of all that I possess.' And the tax collector, standing afar off, would not so much as raise his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast, saying, 'God, be merciful to me a sinner!' I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other; for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted" (Luke 18:11–14).

The Pharisee's prayer is doctrinally accurate. He thanks God. He mentions divine mercy. He lists real sins — extortion, adultery — that he genuinely avoids. His error is not his facts. It is his heart. He uses another man's sin as the measuring stick for his own virtue. The tax collector uses God's holiness as the measuring stick for his own sin. One produces gratitude for relative goodness. The other produces desperation for absolute mercy. Only one went home justified.

Here is the Reformed distinction the parable demands. The tax collector went home justified — not because he felt sufficiently bad, not because his breast-beating earned credit, but because God imputed righteousness to him. The word justified is forensic. It is a courtroom verdict, not an emotional state. The tax collector received a declaration he did not deserve because God applied to him what Another had earned. The Pharisee left with his own righteousness — which, as Isaiah would say, was filthy rags. The tax collector left with Christ's.

The Deceitful Heart

Self-righteousness survives because the human heart is a skilled liar. "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked; who can know it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). Jeremiah asks a rhetorical question. The implied answer: no one can know it — not without outside help. Your heart will tell you that your anger is righteous indignation, that your gossip is concern, that your contempt is discernment, that your refusal to forgive is standing for justice. The heart does not lie randomly. It lies strategically, always in the direction of your self-interest.

This is why self-examination is not optional. It is commanded. "Let each one examine his own work, and then he will have rejoicing in himself alone, and not in another" (Galatians 6:4). Paul does not say "examine another's work." The test is internal. The standard is not "better than him" but "faithful before God." The heart that skips this examination is the heart that assumes its own innocence — which is the Pharisee's fundamental error.

Do you suppose, O man — you who judge those who practice such things and yet do them yourself — that you will escape the judgment of God?

Romans 2:3

God's Kindness Leads to Repentance

Paul presses the point in Romans 2. "Do you suppose, O man... that you will escape the judgment of God? Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?" (Romans 2:3–4). The self-righteous man misreads God's patience. He interprets divine delay as divine indifference. He has not been struck down, so he assumes he is not guilty. The tax collector interpreted divine patience as an invitation to confess. The Pharisee interpreted it as confirmation of innocence.

The difference is everything. Repentance is not a feeling of shame. It is a reorientation of the soul away from self-justification and toward Christ's mercy. "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:8–9). Confession is the anti-Pharisee. It is the moment when you stop performing innocence and start receiving grace.

Paul adds a warning that cuts to the root of performative Christianity: "They profess to know God, but in works they deny Him, being abominable, disobedient, and disqualified for every good work" (Titus 1:16). The profession is verbal. The denial is behavioral. Both can coexist in the same person — and do, in every church, every Sunday. The Pharisee professed to know God better than anyone. Jesus said he denied Him with every prayer.

The Seal of the Foundation

Paul writes to Timothy about the church's foundation, and he names its seal: "Nevertheless the firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: 'The Lord knows those who are His,' and, 'Everyone who names the name of the Lord is to abstain from wickedness'" (2 Timothy 2:19). The seal has two sides. Side one: God knows who belongs to Him. Side two: those who belong to Him abstain from wickedness. Not "perform holiness." Not "manage appearances." Abstain.

The Pharisee would have signed both sides of the seal. He abstained from obvious wickedness. He performed visible holiness. But God knew him — and the knowing was not mutual. The tax collector could not claim either side of the seal by his own record. He beat his breast and begged for mercy. And God knew him. That was enough.

Even conscience testifies against the self-righteous, if he would listen. Paul says the Gentiles "show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness" (Romans 2:14–15). The conscience knows. It accuses or excuses. The self-righteous man has trained his conscience to excuse him by comparing him to worse men. The tax collector let his conscience accuse him — and then ran to the only One who could absolve.

What This Means For You

The Pharisee is not a first-century villain. He is a human type. And he is in your mirror. The moment you read about a fallen leader and feel satisfaction rather than fear — that is the Pharisee. The moment you compare yourself to someone worse and feel relief — that is the Pharisee. The moment you judge another Christian's theology while your own prayer life is empty — that is the Pharisee. The moment you post a moral condemnation online and feel cleaner afterward — that is the Pharisee.

Jesus did not eliminate judgment. He eliminated self-righteous judgment. The person who has removed the plank — who has confessed his own sin, who has received mercy, who knows the depth of his own debt — is the only person qualified to help his brother with the speck. And the help he offers will look like mercy, not a lecture.

The tax collector went home justified. The Pharisee went home still praying. Only one of them knew it. The difference was not their behavior. It was their object. One trusted in himself. One trusted in mercy. Only one went home with what he needed.

The Pharisee is not a first-century villain. He is a human type — and he is in your mirror. The person who has removed the plank does not lecture his brother. He offers mercy, because he has received it. The tax collector went home justified not because he felt bad, but because he begged for mercy and God gave him Christ.
