Lebanon can seize rockets. It cannot seize a stronghold.

Lebanon is attempting to disarm Hezbollah. No government has ever done it. You can seize a weapon. You cannot seize a spiritual stronghold with legislation. The cross already disarmed what parliaments cannot touch.

Persecution Lebanon Romans 13:4 Ephesians 6:12 2 Corinthians 10:4-5 Colossians 2:15

Lebanon's government is attempting to do what no administration has ever accomplished: disarm Hezbollah. The April ceasefire with Israel is fragile — a truce in name only, with continued exchanges of fire. Israel struck a Hezbollah commander in Beirut on May 7. Direct talks between Israel and Lebanon resume in Washington on May 14. The political machinery is moving. The question Scripture raises is whether you can solve with legislation what is fundamentally a spiritual stronghold.[1][2]

Romans 13 is clear about who holds the sword: "For he is God's minister to you for good. But if you do evil, be afraid; for he does not bear the sword in vain; for he is God's minister, an avenger to execute wrath on him who practices evil" (Romans 13:4). The sword is a delegated instrument. The state holds it under God, not beside Him. When a militia seizes the sword and functions as a parallel government — as Hezbollah has for decades, with its own military wing, social services, and political party — it is a theological problem before it is a political one. The Reformed tradition calls it mutiny. Not against the Lebanese constitution. Against the ordinance of God, who appointed civil magistrates to be "under Him" for the public good (Westminster Confession 23.1).

A militia does not need a formal declaration of independence to commit rebellion. It just needs weapons the state cannot take back, territory the state cannot enter, and a constituency that treats its authority as legitimate. Hezbollah has all three. Its weapons are not merely military hardware. They are symbols — of resistance against Israel, of Shiite identity, of power in a fractured sectarian system. You can seize a rocket. You cannot seize a symbol with legislation.

The war behind the war

We do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this age, against spiritual hosts of wickedness in the heavenly places.

Ephesians 6:12

Paul's disclosure in Ephesians 6:12 reframes every disarmament effort: the real enemy is not flesh and blood. The militia fighter is not the primary problem. The principalities that animate him are. "For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds" (2 Corinthians 10:4–5). The word for "stronghold" is ochuroma — a fortress, a castle, a compound defended by walls. Paul is talking about arguments, ideologies, spiritual forces that have constructed fortifications in the human mind. Every militia is a physical manifestation of a spiritual stronghold first.

This is not a call for the church to oppose disarmament. Civil peace is a genuine good, and the state has lawful authority to pursue it. But the church must be the only institution in the conversation that names the real problem: a nation cannot disarm what it did not first arm. The spiritual powers that built Hezbollah's stronghold in Lebanon were not voted in. They were not supplied by Iran alone, though the weapons were. They were constructed in the unseen realm, and they will not be dismantled by a diplomatic framework signed in Washington.

The disarmament that already happened

Colossians 2:15 declares something the Lebanese parliament cannot replicate: "Having disarmed principalities and powers, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them in it" (Colossians 2:15). The verb is apekdusamenos — He stripped them, disarmed them, took their weapons away. The cross was not a defeat that needed a sequel. It was the definitive disarmament of every spiritual power that stands against God. Christ did not negotiate with the principalities. He stripped them. He did not propose a framework for phased disarmament. He triumphed over them publicly.

The Lebanese government can pass laws. It can seize caches. It can sign agreements. Every one of those actions is provisional, limited, and reversible. What Christ accomplished at the cross is not. The church must pray for Lebanon. It must pray for peace. It must reject the lie that the only peace available is the peace of the graveyard — a ceasefire that holds because both sides are exhausted. Shalom is not the absence of artillery fire. It is the presence of the Prince of Peace. And every disarmament that falls short of that is, at best, a shadow of the one that already happened on a hill outside Jerusalem.

Lebanon can seize a rocket. It cannot seize a symbol. The cross disarmed what legislation cannot touch: the spiritual principalities that built the stronghold in the first place.
