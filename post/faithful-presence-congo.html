Faithful Presence When the Ground Moves

Seven million displaced in DR Congo. Pastors, priests, Catholic sisters, and imams going door-to-door in Goma. Not running programs — walking into tents and listening. The church being the church, not waiting for war to end but present in the middle of it. Scripture calls this normal Christianity.

Missions DR Congo Jeremiah 29:4-7 Philippians 1:23-25 Romans 12:1-2 1 Corinthians 15:58 Acts 7:48

May 2026. Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo. Fighting between M23 rebels and government forces continues. Seven million people are internally displaced — more than the population of many countries. The numbers are so large they become abstraction. Seven million. You read it and move on.

But in Goma, faith leaders are not moving on. Pastors, priests, Catholic sisters, and imams are conducting home visits — except there are no homes. They walk through displacement camps. They enter tents. They sit with people whose lives have been reduced to what they could carry. They provide trauma counseling. They mediate disputes. They try, as one report put it, to prevent grief from hardening into violence.

This is not a strategy document. This is not an NGO program with a logframe and measurable outcomes. This is Christians (and, notably, Muslims alongside them) doing what the church has always done in places where the ground has given way: showing up. Staying. Listening. Being present. Scripture calls this normal Christianity. We have made it exceptional.

The Letter From Exile

Jeremiah wrote to people who had lost everything. The Babylonians had taken Jerusalem. The temple was destroyed. The best and brightest of Judah were marched to Babylon at spearpoint. Everything that had defined their relationship with God — the land, the temple, the Davidic king, the sacrificial system — was gone or inaccessible. And Jeremiah wrote them a letter.

The prophet did not tell them to organize a resistance movement. He did not tell them to pray for immediate deliverance. He did not tell them to withdraw into a holy huddle and wait it out. He told them to stay: "Build houses and dwell in them; plant gardens and eat their fruit. Take wives and beget sons and daughters... And seek the peace of the city where I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray to the LORD for it; for in its peace you will have peace" (Jeremiah 29:5–7).

This is astonishing. God's people are in exile, and the word from the Lord is: Build a life there. Seek the welfare of your captors. Pray for the city that destroyed your home. The command to "be faithfully present" is not an accommodation to circumstance. It is a calling. Notice what Jeremiah does NOT say. He does not promise that faithfulness will end the exile quickly. "After seventy years are accomplished at Babylon, I will visit you" (Jeremiah 29:10). Seventy years. Most of the original exiles would die in Babylon. Their grandchildren would see the return. The timeline is God's. The command is theirs. Stay. Build. Pray. Seek peace. You are not where you want to be. Be the church where you are.

Having a desire to depart and be with Christ, which is far better. Nevertheless to remain in the flesh is more needful for you.

Philippians 1:23–24

Paul's Calculus

Paul understood the tension between escape and presence better than anyone. "Hard-pressed between the two," he wrote from prison, "having a desire to depart and be with Christ, which is far better. Nevertheless to remain in the flesh is more needful for you" (Philippians 1:23–24).

Paul was under no illusions about which option was better for him. Death meant Christ's immediate presence. Life meant chains, beatings, shipwrecks, hunger, sleeplessness, and eventually execution. The calculus was clear: depart = far better. Stay = more suffering. And he chose to stay. Not because he was a masochist. Not because suffering is inherently virtuous. Because his presence was "more needful for you" — for the church, for the believers who needed teaching and encouragement and an example of what faithfulness under pressure looks like.

This is the logic of faithful presence. It is not that leaving is always wrong. It is not that suffering is its own reward. It is that the needs of others can make staying the right call even when leaving would be easier, safer, or personally preferable. The church's witness is embodied before it is verbal. You cannot preach "God is with you" while fleeing at the first sign of danger. Paul stayed because his visible scars were part of his proclamation: "From now on let no one trouble me, for I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus" (Galatians 6:17). The scars were not an argument against God's faithfulness. They were evidence of it.

The Portable Temple

The most radical claim in the New Testament about presence might be this: God does not dwell in buildings. "God, who made the world and everything in it, since He is Lord of heaven and earth, does not dwell in temples made with hands" (Acts 17:24). Stephen, moments before his martyrdom, made the same point: "The Most High does not dwell in temples made with hands" (Acts 7:48).

This was fighting talk in a world where every god had a house. The temple in Jerusalem was the center of Jewish identity. To say God does not dwell in any of them was to claim something unprecedented about the nature of divine presence. Then Paul took it further: "Your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19). The church collectively is "a spiritual house" built of "living stones" (1 Peter 2:5). The presence of God is portable. It travels with the people. It shows up in displacement camps and rented halls and borrowed rooms. You cannot bomb the temple anymore. The temple is walking around in the bodies of believers.

This is not abstract theology. The Congo faith leaders going door-to-door in Goma are not bringing God to these people — God is already present in and through them. The church in a displacement camp is not a diminished version of the church in a cathedral. It is the same church, the same presence, the same Spirit. The empire's power is location-dependent. The church's power is Spirit-dependent. One of these is harder to disrupt.

Steadfast, Immovable

Paul ends his great resurrection chapter with a command that feels almost impossible in a displacement camp: "Therefore, my beloved brethren, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Steadfast. Immovable. When the ground is literally moving — when the front lines shift and the camp relocates and the future is unwritable — the command is to be anchored. Not to circumstances. To the resurrection.

The labor is not in vain because Christ is raised. The worst that can happen to you has already been reversed in Him. Death itself is defeated. Everything else is manageable. The Reformed tradition calls this "the hope of heaven" — not wishful thinking but a certainty about the final outcome that reorganizes your relationship to present suffering. If the end of the story is secure, the middle chapters can contain anything. The Congo pastors who walk into tents today do so knowing that the kingdom they serve cannot be shaken, even if the ground beneath them can.

What This Means For You

You are probably not in a displacement camp. The question of faithful presence feels less urgent when the ground isn't moving. But the command applies at every scale.

Faithful presence is the parent who stays engaged with a difficult teenager when it would be easier to check out. It is the spouse who remains in a hard marriage and does the slow work of repair. It is the church member who keeps showing up to a congregation going through conflict, not hopping to the one down the road. It is the employee who refuses to join the gossip culture and instead does good work quietly.

The principle is the same: you are placed where you are placed. The question is whether you will build and plant and seek peace or whether you will treat the present location as a waiting room for something better. The Congo faith leaders show us what this looks like at high resolution. But the resolution you need is in your own life. Where are you tempted to flee? What relationship, responsibility, or place feels too hard to stay in? Paul's calculus is yours to make. Departing to be with Christ is far better. But remaining may be more needful for the people God has placed around you. The labor is not in vain. The ground is held by Another.

The church in a tent is the same church as the church in a cathedral. Same presence. Same Spirit. Same King. You cannot bomb the temple anymore — the temple walks around in the bodies of believers who refuse to flee.
