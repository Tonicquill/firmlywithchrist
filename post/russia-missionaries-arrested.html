Russia called it 'illegal missionary work.' The church called it Sunday.

Four Christians in Kazan were fined for 'illegal missionary work' — praying in a private home without state registration. The church's response is the same one Peter gave the Sanhedrin: we ought to obey God rather than men.

Ecclesiology Russia Acts 5:29 Colossians 1:18 Matthew 16:18 John 18:36 Matthew 22:21 Romans 13:1 Hebrews 12:22-23 2 Timothy 2:19 Hebrews 10:25

Four Christians in Kazan gathered in a private home to pray. They did not apply for state registration. They did not submit their beliefs for bureaucratic review. They did not notify the local authorities that Jesus was the agenda. For this, a Russian court fined them for "illegal missionary work." The charges were real. The Bibles were real. The only thing the state could not see was the church.

The International Union of Evangelical Baptist Christians refuses state registration on principle. They have done so for years. The Russian government calls this defiance. The New Testament calls it normal. "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Peter said this to the Sanhedrin — the highest religious court in Israel — after they commanded the apostles to stop preaching. The apostles did not negotiate. They did not compromise. They stated the limit of earthly authority and kept preaching. Two thousand years later, a Russian magistrate in Kazan discovered the same limit.

The Head of the Church Does Not Need a Visa

The Reformed tradition has never recognized the state's right to license the church. "He is the head of the body, the church, who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead, that in all things He may have the preeminence" (Colossians 1:18). The preeminence is His. The title is His. The office is His. No president, no premier, and no permit office can share it. When a state demands registration before the church can assemble, it is not administering. It is usurping.

Jesus anticipated this. "On this rock I will build My church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18). The promise is not conditional on favorable legislation. The church does not exist because a legislature granted it a charter. It exists because Christ built it. The gates of Hades — not merely the gates of Kazan — will not prevail. A fine is annoying. A prison sentence is costly. Neither is a gate of Hades. Both are temporary.

The Reformers paid for this conviction. John Huss was burned at the stake for teaching that Christ alone is the Head of the church. The Council of Constance offered him safety if he would recant. He refused. The fire was real. The truth was older. The church outlived the council by five centuries. That is the arithmetic of ecclesiology: institutions come and go. The Head remains.

The Limits of Caesar

Paul commands submission to the state: "Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities" (Romans 13:1). But the same Paul, when arrested, appealed to Caesar. The same Peter who wrote "be subject to every ordinance of man" (1 Peter 2:13) also said "we ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). There is no contradiction. There is a boundary.

The state's authority is real but limited. It extends to taxes, roads, courts, and the punishment of evildoers. It does not extend to the conscience, the pulpit, or the prayer meeting in a Kazan living room. Jesus drew this line explicitly: "Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Matthew 22:21). The things that are God's include the church. The state cannot have it. It was never for sale.

Jesus made the boundary sharper still: "My kingdom is not of this world. If My kingdom were of this world, My servants would fight, so that I should not be delivered to the Jews; but now My kingdom is not from here" (John 18:36). Pilate heard this as evasion. The church hears it as definition. The kingdom is not of this world — which means it does not derive its legitimacy from this world's permits, registrations, or legislative majorities. A church that needs state approval to exist is not a church. It is a state department with hymns.

But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, to an innumerable company of angels, to the general assembly and church of the firstborn who are registered in heaven.

Hebrews 12:22–23

The Only Registry That Matters

The state keeps a registry. The church keeps a different one. "The general assembly and church of the firstborn who are registered in heaven" (Hebrews 12:22–23). The only registration that matters is written in the Lamb's Book of Life. No earthly database can add a name to it. No earthly court can remove one. The Kazan believers are registered in heaven whether the Russian state recognizes them or not.

This is why the church does not fear permit denial. "The Word of God is not chained" (2 Timothy 2:9) — Paul wrote this from prison. The cell could chain his body. It could not chain the Word. The Kazan court can fine four believers. It cannot fine the Holy Spirit. The church meets not because a magistrate granted a license but because the King commanded it: "not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together" (Hebrews 10:25). The command is from above. The permit is not.

Paul's seal on the church's foundation is two-sided: "Nevertheless the firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: 'The Lord knows those who are His,' and, 'Everyone who names the name of the Lord is to abstain from wickedness'" (2 Timothy 2:19). Side one: God knows His own. Side two: those who name His name abstain from wickedness. The Russian state knows neither side. It cannot read the registry. It cannot judge the heart. Its fines are administrative. God's recognition is eternal.

What This Means For You

You are probably not in Kazan. You probably do not need a permit to attend church. But the principle travels. Every time a government proposes to register churches, inspect sermons, or approve clergy, it is reaching for a crown that does not fit its head. The church's response should not be outrage. It should be clarity: We will render to Caesar what belongs to Caesar. We will not render what belongs to God.

The Kazan believers paid a fine. They will gather again. The state took their money. It did not take their Head. And that is the arithmetic of Acts 5:29 — the same arithmetic Huss counted at the stake, the same arithmetic the Indonesian church counted when its permit never came, the same arithmetic every believer counts when the state overreaches: obey God rather than men. The sentence is short. The commitment is lifelong. The church outlives every magistrate who ever fined it.

The state fined four believers for praying without a permit. The church will meet again next Sunday — permit or no permit — because its Head does not require registration, its registry is in heaven, and its warranty outlasts every government that ever tried to license it.
