All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness.

2 Timothy 3:16

David Pawson walked up to the pulpit with two working legs. He left it with one. Mid-sermon, a stroke hit him. His body began to shut down. His mouth kept moving. He finished the sermon. Then he was helped off the platform — half-paralyzed, fully spent, and utterly faithful. The British Bible teacher, then in his seventies, had been scheduled to deliver a sobering message warning that England would become Islamic unless the church woke up. The stroke struck his mouth and throat — the very instruments of his craft. The enemy aimed at the preacher's tools. The preacher aimed at obedience. Obedience won.[1]

Paul wrote the definitive word on ministerial mortality: "But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us" (2 Corinthians 4:7). The Greek word for "earthen vessels" is ostrakinos skenos — clay pots, cheap containers, the kind you use for common purposes and discard when they crack. Every preacher is a clay pot. The treasure is the gospel inside. The stroke that hit David Pawson mid-sermon did not touch the treasure. It cracked the pot. The pot kept pouring. This is not a story about grit. It is a story about grace — the kind that is sufficient when the neurons stop firing and the left side goes dark and every natural instinct says stop.

The thorn and the pulpit

My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness. Therefore most gladly I will rather boast in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me.

2 Corinthians 12:9

Pawson did not merely quote this text. He embodied it at the cost of his own motor function. The stroke was a "messenger of Satan" if anything is — an assault on the very faculties a preacher needs to function. Paul "implored the Lord three times that it might depart" from him. Scripture does not tell us whether Pawson prayed for healing before, during, or after. It tells us what he did: he kept preaching. The Reformed tradition, reflecting on the Westminster Confession's teaching that God "sovereignly orders everything He has made" (5.1), understands that the stroke did not interrupt Pawson's sermon. It was part of it. The sermon God ordained for that day included a preacher whose body was failing while his mouth declared the Word. The audience saw a broken pot. What they heard was the treasure.

There is a particular cruelty to a stroke that targets a preacher's mouth. The instrument of his vocation becomes the site of his affliction. Moses protested that he was "slow of speech and slow of tongue" (Exodus 4:10). God used him anyway. Pawson's speech was his instrument for sixty years of teaching. When the stroke hit his mouth and throat, the weapon was aimed with precision. And it missed — not because Pawson was strong, but because the word of God is not chained to the speaker's anatomy. A slurred sermon delivered in obedience outweighs a fluent one delivered in comfort. The kingdom's economy does not grade on elocution.

Not once but twice

Pawson was not done being broken. Around 2015, roughly a dozen years after the first stroke, a transient ischemic attack — a mini-stroke — hit him again. His trustees wondered "whether his ministry had come to an end and that David would be forced to retire on ill health grounds." Around the same time he was diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer that had metastasized to his bones. The pot was cracking further. Pawson took time to recover. Then he went back to the pulpit. "Praise the Lord! HE is faithful," his trustee wrote. "David has improved and is now back in the pulpit with a reduced but occasional itinerary."[2]

The apostle Paul's valediction in 2 Timothy 4:7–8 was not written from a vacation. He wrote it from a Roman prison, awaiting execution, after shipwrecks and beatings and "a thorn in the flesh" he had learned to boast in. The fight is good even when the body is not. The race is finished even when you limp across the line on one functioning leg. The Reformed tradition has a category for this: perseverance of the saints — not the saints' own grip on God but God's grip on the saints. David Pawson kept preaching through two strokes and terminal cancer because the One who called him did not revoke the call when the vessel cracked.

From his nursing home, Pawson completed his final work — a commentary on Colossians. He died on Ascension Day, May 21, 2020, at ninety years old. The timing is nearly too precise. A man who spent his life expounding the Word finished his last commentary and was taken up on the day the church commemorates Christ's ascension to the Father. Providence has a sense of irony. The clay pot finally shattered. The treasure went home.

A stroke hit David Pawson mid-sermon. His body broke. His mouth kept moving. He finished the message, was helped off the pulpit, and later returned to preach again — twice. The pot cracked. The treasure held.
