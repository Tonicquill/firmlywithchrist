China: 31 Christians Jailed in Coordinated Crackdown. Their Faith Was the Evidence.

In Suizhou, Hubei Province, Chinese authorities jailed 31 house church members in what human rights groups call one of the largest coordinated prosecutions of Christians in recent years. Their crime was fellowship. Their evidence was faith.

Persecution Hebrews 10:25 1 Timothy 6:12 Acts 12:5 Hebrews 10:30-31
Read below ↓

All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness.

2 Timothy 3:16

On May 22, a court in Suizhou, Hubei Province, handed down sentences to 31 Christians in what human rights groups describe as one of the largest coordinated prosecutions of believers in recent years. The trials were tightly guarded. Defendants were split into small groups of one or two to prevent them from seeing the full scale of the crackdown. The charge was “using a cult organization to undermine the implementation of the law.” The evidence was fellowship. House church leader Song Yude received four years — the longest sentence. The shortest was two years and four months. Yang Zhijin, a seventy-seven-year-old believer, was jailed for more than three years. His crime was helping detained Christians secure and pay for lawyers. Prosecutors quoted his Christian beliefs as evidence of criminal conduct: “faith in Jesus leads to heaven” and “everyone is sinful and must confess and repent.” The court cited the church's care teams, preaching groups, and young adults ministry as proof of “criminal activity.” Bob Fu, president of ChinaAid, said the verdict was clear: “These are innocent Christians, not criminals. Their only 'crime' is worshipping God outside government control.” The Chinese Communist Party has spent decades trying to make the church state-approved. The church in Suizhou just proved that state approval was never the point.[1]

The author of Hebrews commanded the church: "'Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as is the manner of some, but exhorting one another, and so much the more as you see the Day approaching'" (Hebrews 10:25). The 31 Christians in Suizhou were not forsaking the assembly. They were keeping it — in living rooms, in basement spaces, in the only places an unregistered church can meet in a country that demands registration as the price of permission. The Chinese court called this “cult activity.” The Bible calls it obedience. The care team that visited sick members was not a criminal network. It was the body of Christ being the body. The preaching group that arranged sermons was not a conspiracy. It was the church doing what the church does. The young adults ministry that introduced unmarried Christians to one another was not a recruitment operation. It was the family of God acting like family. The state that calls fellowship a crime has not indicted the church. It has indicted itself.

Fight the good fight of faith, lay hold on eternal life, to which you were also called and have confessed the good confession in the presence of many witnesses.

1 Timothy 6:12

Paul told Timothy: "Fight the good fight of faith, lay hold on eternal life, to which you were also called and have confessed the good confession in the presence of many witnesses" (1 Timothy 6:12). The Suizhou prosecutors understood this verse better than some Christians do. They knew that a confession of faith is dangerous. They knew that “faith in Jesus leads to heaven” is not a private opinion but a public challenge to every state that claims absolute authority. They knew that “everyone is sinful and must confess and repent” undermines the entire ideological foundation of a regime that pretends its leaders are infallible. So they put the confession on trial. They made Song Yude's faith the evidence for his sentence. They made Yang Zhijin's seventy-seven-year-old theology the justification for his three-year imprisonment. They are not the first regime to do this. The Sanhedrin did it to Jesus. Rome did it to the martyrs. And every regime that tries it discovers the same thing: you cannot imprison a confession without advertising it.

Luke recorded what the church did when Peter was imprisoned: "Peter was therefore kept in prison, but constant prayer was offered to God for him by the church" (Acts 12:5). The church in Suizhou is now where Peter was — in prison, but not alone. The church outside Suizhou is where the Jerusalem church was — praying, but not powerless. Bob Fu and ChinaAid are tracking the cases. The international community will issue statements that Beijing will ignore. And the Chinese house church will continue meeting in the same rooms where Song Yude once preached, with the same sermons, the same care teams, and the same young adults introducing unmarried Christians to one another. Because the prison does not end the church. It only proves that the church was never dependent on buildings, or permits, or state approval. The church is dependent on the Head who put all things under His feet — including the Chinese Communist Party.

The writer of Hebrews warned the persecutors: "'Vengeance is Mine, I will repay,' says the Lord... For 'the LORD will judge His people.' It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (Hebrews 10:30-31). The Suizhou court that issued 31 sentences on May 22 did not issue them in a vacuum. It issued them before the eyes of the living God. The prosecutor who quoted “faith in Jesus leads to heaven” as criminal evidence did not realize he was quoting the gospel in a courtroom that will one day be his own. The judge who called care teams “criminal activity” will one day stand before a higher Judge who calls the same activity the evidence of authentic faith. Vengeance is Mine, I will repay. Not “vengeance is the church's.” Not “vengeance is the international community's.” Vengeance is Mine. The God who heard Abel's blood from the ground hears the 31 believers in Suizhou. And the God who numbers the hairs on Song Yude's head numbers the days of the regime that put him in prison. The confession that became evidence in a Chinese courtroom will become evidence in a higher court — and on that day, the prosecutor's indictment will be his own.
