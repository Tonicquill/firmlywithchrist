The state abducted Pastor Raymond Koh. Nine years later, he still speaks.

In 2017, Malaysia's Special Branch abducted Pastor Raymond Koh in broad daylight. His wife still waits. No body. No grave. The Reformed tradition knows what to do with a state that becomes the persecutor and a pastor who paid the ultimate price.

Read below ↓

February 13, 2017. Pastor Raymond Koh steps into his car in Petaling Jaya, Selangor. A fleet of SUVs boxes him in. Men from Malaysia's Special Branch — the state's own intelligence unit — drag him out, throw him into a vehicle, and drive away. Nine years later, his wife Susanna still waits. His children still wait. No body. No grave. No charges. No accountability. The state that Romans 13 calls "God's minister for good" abducted a pastor of Christ's church in broad daylight, and the government's response has been nine years of institutional silence.[1][2]

You have not heard about Raymond Koh. The Western church has been busy. Your algorithm did not surface him. The Malaysian government is counting on exactly this: that nine years is long enough for the world to forget a pastor who made the wrong people uncomfortable. Koh ran a charitable organization that served the poor across religious lines. He was also accused — without trial, without evidence, without due process — of converting Muslims to Christianity. In Malaysia, that accusation is enough to make a man disappear. Not legally. Not justly. But effectively. The machinery of the state closed around him like a fist, and it has not opened since.

God's minister for good — when the text breaks your categories

"Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities," Paul writes. "For there is no authority except from God, and the authorities that exist are appointed by God. For he is God's minister to you for good" (Romans 13:1–4). The Malaysian Special Branch officers who abducted Koh were, by Paul's own framework, "appointed by God." The Westminster Confession of Faith (23.1) teaches that God has "ordained civil magistrates to be under Him" for His glory and the public good. Every confession the Reformed tradition holds says the state bears legitimate authority. None of it says the state's authority is absolute.

When the men who took Raymond Koh stand before the Judge of all the earth, the defense "I was following orders" will carry no weight before the bench of Christ. The apostle Peter, standing before the same kind of state tribunal that took Koh, said the thing that gets pastors killed: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). The Reformed tradition has always understood that state authority is delegated, not sovereign. When a government uses its delegation to persecute the church, it commits what the sources call "nothing short of mutiny" against the Head of the church. The state that abducts a pastor has forfeited the moral warrant it was given. It has become, in the language of Revelation 13, not God's minister but the beast.

The body that never came home

The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up.

1 Samuel 2:6

Psalm 10 is the prayer of a family with no grave to visit: "Why do You stand afar off, O LORD? Why do You hide in times of trouble?" (Psalm 10:1–2). Susanna Koh has lived inside that psalm for nine years. She does not have a death certificate. She does not have remains to bury. She has testimony — eyewitnesses saw the abduction, CCTV captured the vehicles, the license plates traced back to the government — and she has the silence of a state that never had to answer for what it did. The grief of the disappeared is a specific kind of wound. It has no ritual, no closure, no moment when the community gathers and says "it is finished." It is suspended. Indefinite. The theological question is whether the sovereignty of God extends into that suspension.

Hannah's war cry in 1 Samuel 2:6–8 answers: "The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up." The Hebrew verbs are active, personal, direct. God does not merely permit death. He administers it. And He administers resurrection on the other side. The same hand that holds Raymond Koh — wherever he is, in whatever condition, dead or alive — holds the men who took him. No enforced disappearance can derail sovereign providence. God is not confused about where Raymond Koh is. The family may not know. The Special Branch may not say. The God who numbers the hairs of His people did not lose count of this one.

The crime of converting souls

Raymond Koh ran a charity. He served the poor. He crossed religious lines to do it. In Malaysia, where Islam is the state religion and apostasy is a legal and social crisis, a pastor who serves Muslims is a pastor who is watched. The accusation of "conversion" — turning Muslims to Christianity — needs no evidence to destroy a life. The accusation is the weapon. The state is the hand that wields it.

Jesus told His disciples to expect this: "Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul" (Matthew 10:28). Paul told the early churches the same thing after getting stoned and left for dead in Lystra: "We must through many tribulations enter the kingdom of God" (Acts 14:22). Not around the tribulations. Not despite them. Through them. The suffering is the road, not the ditch. The church that calls itself the church militant does not get to be surprised when the battle costs blood. The Reformed tradition identifies the church as an army under orders — and armies take casualties. Koh's disappearance is not a missional failure. It is a missional credential.

The testimony that cannot be erased

Hebrews 11 says something strange about Abel: "By faith he being dead still speaks" (Hebrews 11:4). Abel's body is in the ground. His blood cried out from it. And the author of Hebrews insists he is still speaking. The Malaysian state can make a pastor's body disappear. It cannot make his testimony disappear. Raymond Koh still speaks — through his wife's relentless advocacy, through the witness of his charity, through a case file that the global church may have forgotten but the divine court has not. The blood of the Lamb and the word of their testimony: these are the weapons Revelation says conquer the accuser (Revelation 12:11). Koh wielded both. His disappearance did not disarm him.

The altar of deferred justice

Revelation 6 gives us the only authorized image of what is happening to Raymond Koh right now: "I saw under the altar the souls of those who had been slain for the word of God and for the testimony which they held. And they cried with a loud voice, saying, 'How long, O Lord, holy and true, until You judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?'" (Revelation 6:9–11). The souls under the altar are not passive. They are not "resting in peace." They are crying out for justice, and they are told to wait — not because justice is canceled but because the full number of martyrs is not yet complete. The delay is not divine indifference. It is divine accounting.

The Westminster Confession (5.1) teaches that God "sovereignly orders everything He has made" — every calamity, every crime, every court that fails to prosecute. The Malaysian courts have never held anyone accountable for Raymond Koh's abduction. The Human Rights Commission of Malaysia (SUHAKAM) concluded it was an enforced disappearance by state agents. The government's response was to dismiss the finding. When human courts fail, the divine court does not. Every officer who planned the operation, every agent who drove the SUV, every official who signed off on nine years of silence — they have not escaped the jurisdiction of the Judge. The crime that escapes Malaysian courts will be prosecuted in full at the bench of Christ.

The Malaysian state made a pastor disappear in 2017. Nine years. No body. No charges. No accountability. But the blood of the Lamb and the word of his testimony cannot be erased. He being dead still speaks.
