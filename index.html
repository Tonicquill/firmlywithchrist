When Empires Perform Their Memory

Russia held Victory Day yesterday. No tanks rolled through Red Square. No missiles on display. Soldiers marched on foot while giant screens showed hardware too vulnerable to appear in person. North Korean troops marched for the first time. An empire performed its memory — and looked weaker than it intended. Nations rehearse their glory through power displays. The church remembers through Word and sacrament. These are not the same kind of memory.

The Monument of Pride

May 9, 2026. Moscow's Red Square. The 81st anniversary of the Soviet victory over Nazi Germany. For the first time in nearly two decades, no military hardware rolled through the parade. No Yars missiles. No Su-57 fighters. No nuclear submarines on flatbed trucks. The Defense Ministry cited the "current operational situation" — meaning Ukrainian drone attacks have made it too dangerous to park expensive weapons in one place. Soldiers marched. Screens displayed what could not appear in person.

North Korean troops marched for the first time in Red Square history, honoring Pyongyang's decision to send soldiers to fight alongside Russian forces. A three-day ceasefire with Ukraine was brokered by the United States to coincide with the holiday. Putin spoke for eight minutes.

The whole affair was a nation performing its memory — the Great Patriotic War, the sacrifice, the victory — and the performance showed cracks. The hardware missing. The security unprecedented. The alliance with a pariah state made visible in goose-stepping formation. Empires perform memory through power displays. They always have. The question is what the church does instead.

Scripture has a precise diagnosis for what happens when nations rehearse their glory. It shows up in Daniel, when Nebuchadnezzar surveys Babylon and says: "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for a royal dwelling by my mighty power and for the honor of my majesty?" (Daniel 4:30). The king performs his city to himself. The performance is self-worship.

Earlier still: Babel. "Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth" (Genesis 11:4). The tower was a monument to pride. The purpose was reputation. God's response was confusion.

This is the pattern. Empires build monuments, hold parades, project strength — and the biblical authors call it what it is. Not politics. Idolatry. The performance of power is always, at bottom, a claim about who runs history. Victory Day parades are theological acts. They answer the question "who governs history?" by pointing at tanks and soldiers and saying: We do. Our strength did this. Our memory is the one that matters. The Bible answers differently.

Military Display vs. The Sword of the Spirit

The Philistines gathered against Israel with "thirty thousand chariots and six thousand horsemen, and people as the sand which is on the seashore in multitude" (1 Samuel 13:5). Military display has always been how empires frighten enemies and reassure themselves. The noise, the scale, the visible projection of force — it makes a theological point without saying the words: Our god is stronger than your god.

Paul addresses this directly: "For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds, casting down arguments and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God" (2 Corinthians 10:4–5). The contrast is not that empires have weapons and the church is defenseless. The contrast is that empires wield visible force and the church wields truth. These are different kinds of power operating in different realms with different outcomes. The empire conquers territory that will be lost. The church proclaims a kingdom that cannot be shaken.

The worst atrocity ever committed — the crucifixion of the Son of God — was a convergence of imperial power (Rome), religious authority (the Sanhedrin), and mob sentiment. Every form of human power aligned to kill God. And it was, Peter says at Pentecost, according to "the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23). The greatest display of worldly power in history was God's instrument of salvation disguised as defeat. If that is how God works, then no military parade is what it appears to be. Every tank is borrowed metal. Every soldier's life is held by Another. Every empire's performance is permitted, bounded, and outlasted by the kingdom that advances without firing a shot.

Performing Memory

Here is the crucial distinction. Nations rehearse their memory through festivals and monuments and military processions. The memory is curated. The embarrassing parts are omitted. The glory parts are amplified. Victory Day remembers the Soviet defeat of Nazism. It does not remember the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, the Katyn massacre, the gulag system, or the decades of state-enforced atheism.

God commands His people to remember differently: "Only take heed to yourself, and diligently keep yourself, lest you forget the things your eyes have seen, and lest they depart from your heart all the days of your life. And teach them to your children and your grandchildren" (Deuteronomy 4:9). The church's memory is not nostalgia. It is instruction. Scripture is not a scrapbook — it is a textbook. The acts of God are recounted not to make us feel proud but to make us faithful. "Whatever things were written before were written for our learning, that we through the patience and comfort of the Scriptures might have hope" (Romans 15:4).

National memory selects and distorts. Biblical memory records the full scope — the failures as well as the victories, David's adultery next to David's psalms, Peter's denial next to Peter's sermon at Pentecost. The church's founding documents include the embarrassing parts. This is how you know they are not propaganda. When the church gathers on the Lord's Day, it is performing a different kind of memory. The Lord's Supper is a memorial — "do this in remembrance of Me" — but it is not a parade. There are no tanks. There is bread and wine. The memory is of a death that looked like defeat and was actually triumph. The participants are not displaying strength. They are receiving grace.

The Final Triumph

The biblical authors resolve the noise and tumult of earthly empires by fixating on the consummation of history. "Then the seventh angel sounded: And there were loud voices in heaven, saying, 'The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ, and He shall reign forever and ever!'" (Revelation 11:15). This is not wishful thinking. It is not eschatological escapism. It is the only sane response to watching empires perform their memory while pretending the performance is reality.

Jesus said: "I will build My church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18). The church is on offense. Hades is on defense. The gates cannot hold. Every empire that has ever held a military parade is dust or on its way to dust. The church remains.

What This Means For You

You watched the parade footage. You saw the missing tanks. You heard the speech. The temptation is to process this politically — is Russia weaker or stronger? Is the ceasefire holding? What does this mean for NATO? Those are real questions. They are not the deepest question.

The deepest question is whether you believe the church's memory-keeping or the empire's. One says history is won by the strong. The other says history belongs to the slain Lamb. One says power is displayed in hardware. The other says power is made perfect in weakness. One says the future belongs to whoever has the most missiles. The other says the future has already arrived in the resurrection of Jesus Christ and we are waiting for it to be made visible.

You cannot hold both frames at once. The empire's performance of memory captures the imagination. The church's performance of memory — Word and sacrament, Lord's Day by Lord's Day — retrains it. One of these memories will outlast the other. "The Word of our God stands forever" (Isaiah 40:8). Parades end. Soldiers go home. Screens go dark. "Nevertheless the firm foundation of God stands, having this seal: 'The Lord knows those who are His,' and, 'Everyone who names the name of the Lord is to abstain from wickedness'" (2 Timothy 2:19).

The empire's parade is on borrowed time. The church's memory-keeping — Word and sacrament, Lord's Day by Lord's Day — will outlast every tank that ever rolled through a public square.
