A wife for Isaac

God of Beginnings & Sovereign PurposeOrdinary Time

Now Abraham was old, well advanced in years. And the LORD had blessed Abraham in all things. And Abraham said to his servant, the oldest of his household, who had charge of all that he had, 'I will make you swear by the LORD, the God of heaven and God of the earth, that you will not take a wife for my son from the daughters of the Canaanites, among whom I dwell, but will go to my country and to my kindred, and take a wife for my son Isaac.'

Genesis 24:1-4

Genesis 24 is the longest chapter in Genesis — sixty-seven verses about finding a wife for Isaac. At first glance, it seems out of place. Genesis 22 is the theological climax of the Abraham narrative. Genesis 23 records Sarah's death. And then Genesis 24 is... a matchmaking story? But the length is intentional. Genesis 24 is a masterclass in ordinary providence — the doctrine that God governs every detail of daily life, including wells, camels, and marriage proposals. The servant's prayer is specific: "Let the young woman to whom I shall say, 'Please let down your jar that I may drink,' and who shall say, 'Drink, and I will water your camels' — let her be the one whom You have appointed for Your servant Isaac" (Genesis 24:14). Before he finishes speaking, Rebekah arrives. Not coincidence. Providence.

Watering ten camels is not a small request. A thirsty camel drinks up to twenty-five gallons. Ten camels, two hundred fifty gallons — this is hours of work. The servant's test is not random. It reveals character: the woman God appointed would be generous, industrious, and hospitable. Rebekah passes immediately. The servant's response is worship: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of my master Abraham, who has not forsaken His steadfast love and His faithfulness" (Genesis 24:26-27). Recognizing providence leads to worship. Missing providence leads to anxiety. The same God who orchestrated Rebekah's arrival at the well orchestrates your life with equal precision.

Rebekah's willingness to leave her family and go to Canaan echoes Abraham's original call: leave everything and go to a land you do not know. She becomes the second matriarch of the covenant line, the mother of Jacob and Esau. The ordinary has eternal consequences. Marriage is not merely a human institution. It is the mechanism through which the covenant promise passes from generation to generation. God uses wells and weddings and water jars. The Westminster Confession (5.1) teaches that God "governs all His creatures and all their actions." Including camels. Including marriage. Nothing is too ordinary for God's extraordinary governance.

Life Lesson

God is at work in the mundane details — who you marry, where you live, what you do. Nothing is too ordinary for His orchestration. Pray over your decisions, but trust the One who answers before you finish speaking.
