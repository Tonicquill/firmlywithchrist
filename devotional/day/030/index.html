Day 30 of 365 · January

Baptism of Christ

God of Beginnings & Sovereign PurposeOrdinary Time

Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to John, to be baptized by him. John would have prevented Him, saying, 'I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?' But Jesus answered him, 'Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness.' And when Jesus was baptized, immediately He went up from the water, and behold, the heavens were opened to Him, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming to rest on Him; and behold, a voice from heaven said, 'This is My beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased.'

Matthew 3:13-17

Matthew 3:13-17 records one of the most theologically perplexing moments in the Gospels: Jesus comes to John to be baptized, and John objects. John's baptism was "a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4). Jesus had no sin. John knew it. "I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?" Jesus' reply is the interpretive key to His entire ministry: "Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness." The phrase is dense. Jesus is not being baptized for righteousness, as if He lacked it. He is being baptized to fulfill righteousness — to stand where sinners stand, to identify with the people He came to save. The baptism is the beginning of His public identification with sinners that will culminate at the cross, where He who knew no sin becomes sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). The descent into the Jordan is a preview of the descent into the grave.

The heavens are torn open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father speaks: "This is My beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased." This is the clearest Trinitarian moment in the Gospels. The Son is in the water. The Spirit descends on the Son. The Father speaks over the Son. All three Persons of the Godhead are present and distinguishable. And the Father's declaration combines two Old Testament texts: Psalm 2:7 ("You are My Son") and Isaiah 42:1 ("My chosen, in whom My soul delights"). The first is a royal coronation psalm. The second is the opening of the first Servant Song. Jesus is simultaneously the King and the Servant — the royal Son enthroned over the nations and the suffering Servant who will bear the sins of the nations. The Father is pleased with the Son before the Son has done any public work. The Father's pleasure is not earned. It is eternal. And it is the pleasure the Son extends to everyone united to Him by faith.

The descent of the Spirit like a dove is rich with Genesis echoes. The Spirit hovered over the waters of creation (Genesis 1:2). After the flood, Noah sent out a dove that returned with an olive leaf — proof that the waters of judgment had receded (Genesis 8:11). At Jesus' baptism, the Spirit-dove descends on the One who will pass through the waters of judgment and emerge as the new creation. The old world was judged by water. The new creation is inaugurated by water — water in which the Creator Himself stands, consecrating it as the sign of a new covenant. Christian baptism looks back to the Jordan. But the Jordan looks forward to the cross. And the cross is where identification becomes substitution.

Life Lesson

Jesus did not stand apart from human need. He stood in the water with sinners. He did not need baptism. He chose it. Your baptism is not a testimony to your holiness. It is a testimony to Christ's willingness to identify with you.
